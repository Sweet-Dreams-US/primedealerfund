Every car dealership sits on land and in a building, and someone owns both. Sometimes the dealer owns them. Sometimes a landlord does, and the dealer pays rent. This choice affects how much cash a dealer has tied up, how much risk the business carries, and how easy it is to grow or sell. This article explains the two arrangements, the main tradeoffs, and the questions worth asking when you look at any dealership.

## The two arrangements

When a dealer owns the real estate, the dealer holds the land and the building. The purchase is usually financed. Willie Beck of Capital Automotive writes that owned dealership property is typically financed with a bank or captive finance loan that requires a 20 to 25 percent down payment, on terms of 5 to 10 years. The dealer's equity is the property's fair market value minus what is still owed on the loan.

When a dealer leases, a separate landlord owns the real estate and the dealer pays rent to use it. A common version is the sale-leaseback, where a dealer who already owns the property sells it to an investor and leases it back. The dealer keeps operating the franchise at the same site.

## What a dealership lease usually looks like

The details differ from deal to deal, but the sources describe some common features.

Capital Automotive, a company that buys dealership property and leases it back, says its initial base terms are generally 20 years, with renewal at the dealer's election, so a total term can reach 40 years or more. James Mitchell of Cushman & Wakefield describes a standard lease as an initial term of 15 years with renewal options of 5 to 10 years each. Terms vary, and these are only examples from two professionals who work in this field.

Capital Automotive also explains how rent is set. It applies a cap rate to the property's value, and the cap rate depends on items such as the 10-year Treasury yield, the dealer's profitability, the strength of the franchise and the length of the lease. Rent increases are tied to the Consumer Price Index or set as fixed changes.

:::note Worth knowing
Capital Automotive defines a triple net (NNN) lease as one where the tenant is responsible for property taxes, insurance costs and regular maintenance costs.
:::

## The case for owning

Owning gives control. In the WardsAuto article by John Davis, a dealer who prefers ownership says he values that control and sees the property as an estate planning tool. He finds real estate easier to pass to children than dealership ownership, which can face factory approval requirements.

Beck also notes that a dealer who owns the property and later sells the business can let the buyer choose whether to buy or lease the property, which keeps flexibility in the sale.

The cost is capital. Owning ties up money in a building rather than in the business. Davis's article says ownership typically needs up to 20 percent equity, and real estate loans often come with personal guarantees or liens on business assets that can reduce borrowing capacity.

## The case for leasing

Leasing frees capital. According to Davis's article, leasing lowers the total investment risk of a dealer and lets the dealer concentrate on franchise operations. Money that would have gone into real estate can go into additional dealerships or other businesses.

Leasing also changes how a dealer deals with facility changes. The same article points out that automakers' demands for updated facilities raise costs, and a lease with an expiration date lets a dealer leave a site that has become too small, poorly located or out of date. It also notes that a single-purpose building, such as a dealership, can be difficult to market in a weak economy.

Mitchell adds that leasing keeps the real estate off the balance sheet, which improves return on capital.

:::note Worth knowing
Leasing has its own risk. Mitchell writes that after the 2008 financial crisis, landlords were unwilling to grant temporary rent relief, and tenants with fixed rent faced serious problems when operating profit fell.
:::

## Selling the property and leasing it back

A sale-leaseback converts the equity in a building into cash while the dealer keeps running the dealership. Beck lists several uses for that cash: buying additional franchises, buying new properties without a 20 to 25 percent down payment, and investing in other assets. He says a dealer keeps the franchise and control of the site, and that this can be useful for estate planning.

Beck also advises dealers to talk to their advisors about the tax, legal and other effects before going ahead. Beck notes that when refinancing rates are attractive, a sale-leaseback may only make sense if the dealer needs to free the equity.

Capital Automotive adds that, in its view, a higher overall value may sometimes be reached by selling the real estate and the dealership operations separately. That is the view of a company that buys real estate, so a reader should treat it as one perspective.

## Questions to ask about any dealership

If you are studying a dealership business as an outside observer, the owned or leased question leads to practical follow-ups:

- Does the business own the land and building, or does it pay rent to a landlord?
- If it leases, what is the term, and how does rent change over time?
- Does the lease include options to renew or to buy the property?
- If there is a loan on the property, what does it require of the dealer?
- How would rent or loan payments hold up if profit fell?

There is no single right answer. Davis's article suggests a dealer's risk tolerance and growth strategy decide which arrangement fits, and that an aggressive growth plan is more likely to call for leasing.
