Walk onto almost any used car lot in the United States and look at the windows. Each car carries a Buyers Guide, a form required by the Federal Trade Commission. It is easy to ignore and it is one of the most useful pieces of paper in a used car sale. This article explains what the Guide says, what it controls, and why it matters to buyers and to anyone who follows the dealership business.

## What the Buyers Guide is

The FTC says dealers have to display a Buyers Guide in every used car they offer for sale. Dealers must also give the Guide to the buyer after the sale. The rule covers light-duty vans and trucks, demonstrators and program cars. Dealers do not have to display it on motorcycles or most recreational vehicles.

The point of the Guide is to put the key terms of the sale in one standard place before you sign anything. You do not have to ask for it and the dealer does not have to be persuaded to show it.

## What the Guide tells you

The Guide lists the car's major mechanical and electrical systems and some of the major problems to watch for. Most important, it states whether the car is sold "as is" or with a warranty. If there is a warranty, the Guide says what percentage of repair costs the dealer will pay.

That makes it a quick comparison tool. If you are looking at two similar cars on two different lots, the Guides tell you which one comes with dealer coverage and how much of a repair the dealer will pay for.

## What "as is" means

When a car is sold "as is," the "As Is" box on the Guide is checked and the Guide says there is no dealer warranty. The dealer will not pay for problems or repairs after the sale, so you bear that risk. A used car can still be a good purchase this way, but it changes what a pre-purchase inspection is worth to you.

If the Guide shows a warranty instead, the dealer must provide it, even if the sales contract says "as is."

:::note Worth knowing
The FTC states that the Buyers Guide "will override anything in your sales contract." If you negotiate changes to the warranty, the dealer should reflect them in both the Guide and the contract.
:::

## Implied warranties

A car that has no written warranty still carries implied warranties, unless it is sold "as is." The FTC describes two. A warranty of merchantability means the car will run. A warranty of fitness for a particular purpose applies when the dealer recommends a vehicle for a specific use. The FTC says implied warranty coverage can last as long as four years, but the length varies by state.

This is why the "as is" box matters so much. Checking it is how a dealer sells a car without those implied warranties.

## Using the Guide to compare cars

Because every dealer must display the same form, you can line up the Guides from several cars and compare them directly. Look first at whether each car is sold as is or with a warranty. For a car with a warranty, compare the percentage of repair costs the dealer will pay. Then compare the lists of major systems and problems to watch for with what you see when you inspect the car. The Guide does not replace an inspection, but it tells you which questions to ask.

If the Guide on the window and the contract you are handed disagree, the Guide controls, and you should ask the dealer to correct the contract.

## State rules also apply

The Guide's requirements are federal, and states add rules of their own. The FTC notes that dealers in Maine and Wisconsin display their own version of the Buyers Guide. For "as is" disclosures and cancellation rights where you live, the FTC points buyers to their state attorney general.

So the Guide is a floor, not the whole picture. Check your own state's rules before you decide what a "yes" at the finance desk means.

## Put promises in writing

The Guide itself reminds buyers that spoken promises are difficult to enforce. If a verbal promise matters to you, such as a repair the dealer will make or a cancellation right, ask for it to be written on the Guide.

The FTC gives related advice. Get add-on prices and details in writing. Get the out-the-door price in writing before you visit the dealer. A written number is easy to compare and hard to misremember.

## A quick checklist for the lot

Before you talk price, find the Guide on the window. Check whether the "As Is" box is marked. If a warranty is listed, read what percentage of repair costs the dealer pays. Look at the list of major systems and problems to watch for. Ask for every promise in writing, and ask for the out-the-door price in writing too. Keep your copy of the Guide after the sale.
