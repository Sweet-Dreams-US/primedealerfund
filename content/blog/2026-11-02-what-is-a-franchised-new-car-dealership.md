When you drive past a store with a Nissan, Chevrolet or Ford sign, you are often looking at an independent business, not a showroom run by the automaker. That business sells the brand's new cars under a contract called a franchise agreement. Understanding that arrangement is the first step to understanding how the auto retail industry works.

This article is for readers new to the industry. It explains what a franchised dealership is, what the franchise agreement usually covers, and why state law plays a large role.

## The basic arrangement

A car dealership is a business that sells new or used cars at retail, typically under a franchise agreement with an automaker or its authorized distributor. Dealerships are usually franchised to sell and service the vehicles of specific companies.

In the terms used for any franchise, the dealer is the franchisee and the automaker is the franchisor. The dealer sells a product it did not design. The automaker relies on the dealer's investment in buildings and people, and on the dealer's local relationships, to reach customers.

## What a franchise agreement covers

Public dealer groups describe their franchise agreements in their annual reports. AutoNation files a yearly Form 10-K with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. In its report for fiscal year 2025, the company describes its agreements this way:

- **Term.** The agreements run for various lengths, from one year to no expiration date. The company says it expects them to continue and anticipates routine renewals where the terms are finite.
- **Territory.** Each agreement gives a non-exclusive right to sell the manufacturer's or distributor's brand of vehicles within a set geographic area.
- **Facilities.** The agreements set operating requirements for showroom and service facilities, signage and personnel. Manufacturers may also set minimum facility requirements that can require significant capital spending.
- **Termination.** A manufacturer may end or decline to renew an agreement for several causes, including performance deficiencies in sales volume, sales effectiveness and customer satisfaction.

These are AutoNation's own descriptions of its agreements. Other dealers and other brands will have their own terms, so anyone studying a particular store needs to read that store's agreement.

## Where state law comes in

Dealer franchises are also shaped by state law. In its 10-K, AutoNation says state dealership franchise laws generally make it unlawful for a manufacturer to end or decline to renew a franchise unless good cause exists. The company describes these laws as difficult for manufacturers to overcome outside of bankruptcy.

The general reference article on car dealerships adds that dealer licenses can be limited to a geographic area, and that most states restrict new dealerships that would compete with an existing one of the same brand. It also says that state laws in the United States prohibit manufacturers from selling directly to consumers.

The details vary by state, so the law of the state where a store operates is part of understanding that store.

## How a franchised store gets its vehicles

According to the same 10-K, dealerships acquire new vehicles for retail sale either directly from the manufacturer or distributor, or through trades with other stores of the same brand. Used vehicles come mainly from customers, through trade-ins, and vehicles that do not sell at retail are typically sold wholesale at auction.

A franchised store also provides maintenance, repair and collision repair, including manufacturer recall and warranty work. It arranges customer financing, mainly through third-party lenders, for a commission, and sells vehicle protection products.

## Why both sides depend on each other

The general reference article on car dealerships describes the relationship as one that can benefit both sides. The dealer gets a product people already want to buy. The automaker gets a seller who has paid for facilities and built a local reputation.

The same article also notes that either side can act opportunistically once the other has sunk money into the business. For example, the automaker might push for low vehicle prices, or the dealer might use its position in the local market to overcharge. Written terms on facilities, performance and termination, along with state law, are part of how the two sides manage that risk.

The 10-K shows the same link from the dealer's side. A manufacturer's standards for showrooms, signage and personnel mean the dealer's spending is tied to what the automaker requires, and the performance standards mean the dealer's sales results are measured against the automaker's expectations.

## What to take from this

Three points are worth remembering. First, the dealer is an independent business that sells a brand under contract. Second, the contract covers term, territory, facilities and performance, and each of those affects how the store is run. Third, state law limits when a manufacturer can end or decline to renew a franchise without good cause.
