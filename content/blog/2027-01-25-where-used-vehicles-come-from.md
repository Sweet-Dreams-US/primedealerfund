Every used car on a dealer's lot arrived there from somewhere. A dealership cannot sell a used vehicle it does not own, so finding good ones at the right price is one of the main jobs in running a used car department.

This article looks at where those vehicles come from, how each source works, and why sourcing matters to the business. It is part of a series on how auto retail works. It is general education about the industry.

## Why used vehicles matter to a dealership

A franchised dealership earns money from more than one activity. The National Automobile Dealers Association (NADA) reports a breakdown of dealership sales dollars for 2025. Used vehicles made up 31.8 percent of total dealership sales dollars. New vehicles were 54.9 percent, and service and parts were 13.3 percent.

That puts used vehicles at nearly a third of sales dollars. The NADA report also shows the share was 32.0 percent in 2024, so it stayed steady from one year to the next.

Because used vehicles are such a large part of the business, the way a dealer fills the used lot is an important operating decision.

:::note The 2025 sales mix
According to the 2025 NADA Data Full-Year Report, used vehicles were 31.8 percent of dealership sales dollars, new vehicles 54.9 percent, and service and parts 13.3 percent.
:::

## The main sources

NADA tracks where dealers got the used vehicles they retailed. Its report groups the sources into five categories: trade-ins on a new vehicle, auction purchases, street purchases, trade-ins on a used vehicle, and other.

The sections below describe each one in plain terms.

## Trade-ins

A trade-in happens when a customer gives a dealership their current vehicle as part of buying another one. NADA separates trade-ins on a new vehicle from trade-ins on a used vehicle. Either way, the dealership receives a vehicle without having to go out and find it.

Trade-ins are attractive for several reasons. The dealer knows where the vehicle came from. The dealership also earns on the sale of the next vehicle at the same time.

AutoNation, a large U.S. dealership group, says in its annual report for fiscal year 2025 that it generally acquires used vehicles from customers, primarily through trade-ins and its "We'll Buy Your Car" program.

## Buying directly from customers

Not every customer who sells a vehicle is buying another one. Some dealers make offers to people who only want to sell. NADA lists this as a street purchase.

AutoNation describes its version of this. Its "We'll Buy Your Car" program lets it self-source a significant portion of its used vehicle inventory. The company says customers receive a guaranteed trade-in offer honored for 7 days or 500 miles at any of its locations.

The appeal is the same as with trade-ins. The dealer is buying from the public instead of from a wholesale market, which gives it more control over what it acquires.

## Auctions

An auction is a wholesale marketplace where dealers buy and sell vehicles among themselves and with other sellers. NADA lists auction purchases as a distinct source of used vehicles.

AutoNation names auctions as another source of used vehicles, along with lease terminations and other sources. A lease termination is a vehicle that comes back at the end of a lease, which the finance company or dealer may then sell.

Auctions also work in the other direction. AutoNation says used vehicles that it does not sell at its own stores are generally sold at wholesale through auctions. A dealer can therefore use an auction both to find vehicles and to move vehicles that do not fit its own lot.

:::note Reconditioning comes next
AutoNation says the used vehicles it acquires are generally reconditioned in its parts and service departments before they are sold at retail.
:::

## From acquisition to the lot

Getting a vehicle is the first step. Before it can be sold, it usually needs inspection, repair and cleaning. AutoNation's report says its acquired used vehicles are generally reconditioned in the parts and service departments before retail sale.

This links two parts of the dealership. The service department does more than repair customer vehicles. It also helps prepare inventory for sale. A dealership with a strong service operation can recondition its own used vehicles instead of relying only on outside shops.

The cost of acquiring and reconditioning a vehicle, compared with the price it sells for, is a central question in used vehicle retailing.

## Why sourcing matters

Sourcing affects several things at once.

Inventory quality. A vehicle taken in on trade has a known origin. An auction vehicle may need closer inspection.

Cost. Each source has a different price pattern. A dealer that can fill its lot through trade-ins and direct purchases depends less on wholesale prices.

Speed. A used lot with a steady supply of vehicles keeps selling. A dealer that runs short has to buy more at auction to fill the gap.

Customer relationships. Trade-ins and direct purchases bring customers through the door, which can also lead to service visits and future sales.

For anyone studying the industry, the key point is that a used car department is a sourcing business as well as a selling business. The vehicles a dealer can acquire and prepare, and the cost of doing so, shape what the department can earn.
