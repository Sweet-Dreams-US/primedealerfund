A franchised dealership sells two kinds of vehicles, and they do not earn money in the same way. New vehicles bring in most of the sales dollars. Used vehicles carry a different margin and a different cost to get on the lot. Published 2025 numbers show how far apart the two lines are, and why no single figure tells you which one matters more.

## Start with how much of the business each line is

The National Automobile Dealers Association publishes yearly data on U.S. franchised dealerships. In its 2025 full-year report, one chart shows each department's share of total dealership sales dollars. New vehicles were 54.9 percent, used vehicles were 31.8 percent, and service and parts were 13.3 percent.

These are shares of sales dollars. They are not shares of profit. A department can bring in a large part of the sales and a small part of the profit, or the other way around. The NADA report does not break out gross profit by department, so to see profit we need a dealer group that reports it.

## What one large public dealer group reports

AutoNation is a public company, so its annual filing and its earnings release list revenue and gross profit by line. For the full year 2025, new vehicle revenue was $13,501.3 million and new vehicle gross profit was $664.8 million. That is a gross margin of 4.9 percent.

Used vehicle revenue was $7,814.0 million and used vehicle gross profit was $462.6 million. Those used totals include wholesale sales. Dividing the two gives about 5.9 percent. For retail used vehicles alone, the company reported revenue of $7,269.1 million and gross profit of $419.2 million, a margin of 5.8 percent.

So on a percentage basis, used vehicles earned a little more than new vehicles for this company in 2025. That is not the whole story, because a percentage says nothing about how many dollars each car leaves behind.

:::note Worth knowing
AutoNation reported gross profit of $2,564 per new vehicle retailed and $1,555 per used vehicle retailed for full year 2025. The used margin is higher, but the profit per car is lower.
:::

## Percent margin and dollars per vehicle are different questions

How can a higher margin produce fewer dollars per car? The answer is the price of the car. The same company retailed 259,264 new vehicles in 2025. Dividing new vehicle revenue by that count gives roughly $52,000 of revenue per new vehicle. For used, $419.2 million of retail gross profit at $1,555 per vehicle works out to about 269,600 retail units, and $7,269.1 million of retail revenue over that count is roughly $27,000 per vehicle. These per-vehicle revenue figures are our own arithmetic from the reported totals, so treat them as approximate.

A 4.9 percent margin on a car near $52,000 is a bigger dollar amount than a 5.8 percent margin on a car near $27,000. That matches the reported $2,564 and $1,555. The two lines are measured in the same units, but the vehicles they sell are priced very differently.

The practical point is to keep both measures in view. Margin shows how much of each sales dollar is kept. Profit per vehicle shows what one sale is worth. A dealer or an investor who reads only one of them can reach the wrong conclusion about the other line.

## How used vehicles get onto the lot

New and used inventory also differ in how the dealer comes to hold it. In its 2025 annual filing, AutoNation says it generally acquires used vehicles from customers, primarily through trade-ins and its "We'll Buy Your Car" program. It also buys through auctions, lease terminations, and other sources.

The same filing says the company generally reconditions used vehicles for retail sale in its own parts and service departments. Buying the vehicle at the right price and then preparing it are two steps that are part of the used vehicle line. What the dealer pays and what it spends on reconditioning both sit between the purchase and the final sale price, so they are part of what shapes the profit on each used unit.

## Vehicle sales are only part of the picture

Looking at new and used alone leaves out the departments that sit behind the sale. In the same AutoNation results, parts and service produced $2,355.1 million of gross profit on $4,835.4 million of revenue, a gross margin of 48.7 percent. Finance and insurance, net, produced $1,464.4 million, or $2,769 per vehicle retailed.

New and used gross profit together came to $1,127.4 million, so parts and service alone earned a little more than twice that. Compare this with the NADA chart, where service and parts were 13.3 percent of sales dollars. A department's share of sales and its share of profit can be far apart.

:::note Worth knowing
The figures in this article come from one public dealer group and from a national trade association's yearly data. Other dealerships report different mixes, margins, and profit per vehicle.
:::

## What this means when you look at a dealership

When you read about a dealership or a group of dealerships, a few questions follow from the numbers above. How much of the business is new, and how much is used? What is the gross profit per vehicle for each, not just the margin? How does the group acquire and prepare its used inventory? How large are the service and finance and insurance departments compared with vehicle sales?

None of these questions has one correct answer, and they will change from one dealership to the next. Asking them is a way to read the published data without leaning on a single number.
