Most people think of a car dealership as a place that sells cars. A large part of what happens on the property has nothing to do with a sale. It happens in the service drive, the repair bays and the parts counter. That department is called parts and service, and it is a business line of its own.

This article explains what the department does, what kinds of work it handles, and how one public dealer group describes it in its annual report.

## What parts and service means

Parts and service is the part of a dealership that repairs and maintains vehicles and sells the parts used to do it. Some dealer groups also call it after-sales, because it serves a customer after the vehicle has been sold.

The department is made up of a service side, where technicians work on vehicles, and a parts side, which supplies those technicians and can also sell to outside buyers. It sits next to the other departments in a dealership, such as new vehicle sales, used vehicle sales and the finance and insurance office.

## Four kinds of service work

Service work is not all the same. The type of work decides who pays for it and who is allowed to do it. AutoNation, the large public dealer group, describes several types in its annual report on Form 10-K for fiscal year 2025.

**Warranty and service contract work.** AutoNation says its stores perform warranty and service contract work on vehicles covered by manufacturer warranties and service contracts, and that they direct bill the manufacturer. The customer does not pay the shop in this case. The manufacturer does.

**Recall repairs.** The filing says its stores provide manufacturer recall repairs and other warranty work that can be performed only at franchised dealerships. A franchised dealership is one with an agreement with a vehicle manufacturer to sell and service its brand.

**Customer-pay work.** The company also provides what it calls customer-pay service work. This is the routine maintenance and repair a vehicle owner pays for directly.

**Collision repair.** AutoNation reports that it operated 52 AutoNation-branded collision centers as of December 31, 2025.

:::note From the filing
AutoNation's 2025 Form 10-K describes the parts and service business as higher-margin, and says it has historically been less sensitive to macroeconomic conditions than new and used vehicle sales.
:::

## The parts side of the business

Parts are more than something the technicians use. According to the same filing, AutoNation runs separate wholesale parts operations that sell parts to collision repair shops and independent repair providers. It also operates a website, AutoNationParts.com, that sells parts and accessories to consumers.

The filing lists three parts distribution centers as of December 31, 2025. It also says its parts and service departments recondition used vehicles and handle preparatory work on new vehicles. So the department also supports the sales side. A used car is not ready to sell until someone has inspected and repaired it.

## Why the department matters to the whole dealership

Two features stand out from AutoNation's description.

First, the work is repeat business. A vehicle needs service many times over its life, while a sale happens once. A dealership that builds a loyal service customer base keeps seeing customers long after the sale.

Second, the filing says this business has historically been less sensitive to economic conditions than vehicle sales. Sales can rise and fall with the economy, interest rates and the supply of vehicles. People still need their vehicles repaired.

Be careful with this point. It is how one company describes its own history. It does not promise that the same will hold for another dealer group or in a future year.

## What to ask when you study a dealership

If you are trying to understand a dealership or a dealer group, the parts and service department gives you several useful questions:

- How much of the business is warranty and recall work, which a manufacturer pays for, compared with customer-pay work?
- Does the group have its own collision centers or wholesale parts operations?
- How does the group report the department? In AutoNation's case the filing says parts and service is not a separate reportable segment, so you will not find it on a line of its own in segment reporting.
- Does the group say how much revenue and gross profit each department contributes? The filing refers to the contribution of each line to total revenue and gross profit, and the detail is where an analyst should look.

Questions like these turn a general statement such as "service is a steady business" into something you can check against a company's own numbers.

## A word on reading what companies say about themselves

Everything in the previous section comes from a company describing its own business. That is a useful primary source, but it is written by the company. Read the statements as the company's account, check the numbers in the full report, and compare with other dealer groups before drawing a conclusion.

Prime Dealer Fund's own site also says that projected returns are targets only and are not guaranteed. The same caution applies to any statement about how steady a business line is.

## The takeaway

Parts and service is a separate business line inside a franchised dealership. It covers warranty and service contract work paid by manufacturers, recall repairs, customer-pay work and, at some groups, collision repair and wholesale parts. One large public dealer group calls it a higher-margin business that has been less sensitive to economic swings than vehicle sales. To judge any single dealership, look at the mix of work, how it is reported, and the numbers in the full filing.
