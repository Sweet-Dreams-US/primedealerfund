When people talk about how big a car dealership is, the first number they usually hear is units. A unit is one vehicle sold. It sounds almost too simple to need an explanation, yet the word shows up in dealer reports, industry data and public company filings, and it is easy to misread if you do not know what is being counted.

This article explains what a unit of sale is, how dealers split units into groups, and why a unit count is only one piece of the picture. It is written for people who are learning how auto retail works, including investors who want to read dealer numbers with more confidence.

## A unit is one vehicle

In a dealership, a unit is a single vehicle that changes hands. One car sold is one unit. Ten trucks sold in a month is ten units. The count does not care whether the vehicle cost $20,000 or $90,000. A compact car and a luxury SUV each count as one.

That is the main reason dealers like the measure. It is easy to count, it is easy to compare from month to month, and it does not move around when prices rise or fall. If a store sold 100 vehicles last month and 110 this month, the unit count shows the change right away.

## New units and used units

Dealers usually split units into two groups. New units are vehicles that have not been sold to a retail owner before. Used units are vehicles that have had a previous owner, whether they came from a trade-in, an auction or another source.

The split matters because the two groups behave differently. New vehicle sales depend on what the manufacturer ships to the dealer. Used vehicle sales depend on what the dealer can acquire and recondition. The industry's yearly data keeps the two groups apart for this reason, and so do public dealer filings.

## Retail units and fleet units

Inside the new vehicle group there is another split. A retail unit is a vehicle sold to an individual or a business buyer who walks in as a customer. A fleet unit is a vehicle sold in a larger order to a company or agency that buys many vehicles at once, such as a business that keeps a group of vehicles in service.

You can see this split in how the numbers are reported. The 2025 annual report of AutoNation, a large public dealer group, lists "Retail New Vehicle Unit Sales" as its own line. When it describes the national market, it says that approximately 16.3 million new vehicles, "including retail and fleet vehicles," were sold in the United States in 2025. The same year can therefore show two different totals depending on whether fleet sales are included. When you read a unit count, check which one it is.

:::note Worth knowing
The 2025 NADA Data report says the nation's 16,990 franchised light-vehicle dealers sold 16.2 million light-duty vehicles, which works out to an average of 955 new vehicles per dealership for the year.
:::

## What the national numbers show

The NADA report is a good place to see units at national scale. It reports 16,233,363 light-duty vehicles sold in 2025. That total is made up of 2,755,548 new cars and 13,477,815 light-duty trucks. The report counts light-duty trucks together, which includes pickups, SUVs and vans, and they made up most of the total.

The same report gives the average retail selling price for a new vehicle in 2025 as $48,205, up from $47,652 in 2024. Two vehicles can be one unit each and still be very different in dollar terms, which leads to the next point.

## A unit count is not revenue and not profit

Units tell you how many vehicles were sold. They do not tell you how much money the dealer took in, and they say even less about how much it kept.

Consider AutoNation's 2025 report. It shows $13,501.3 million of new vehicle revenue from 259,264 retail new vehicle units. Dividing one by the other gives roughly $52,000 of revenue per unit. That is a calculation from the filing, not a figure the company reports itself, and it blends domestic, import and premium luxury brands. A dealer that sells mostly luxury vehicles will show a higher revenue per unit than one that sells mostly entry-level models, even if the unit counts are the same.

Revenue is also different from profit. Dealers earn money in several ways: selling new vehicles, selling used vehicles, arranging financing and insurance, and servicing vehicles after the sale. AutoNation's filing describes its finance and insurance profit on a per vehicle retailed basis, which shows that a single unit can lead to income beyond the sale price itself. The NADA report shows how sales dollars were divided across the dealer's main areas in 2025: new vehicles 54.9 percent, used vehicles 31.8 percent, and service and parts 13.3 percent. Finance and insurance is not broken out in that table.

## How to read a unit count

Here are a few habits that help when you see a unit figure.

First, ask what kind of unit it is: new, used, retail, fleet, or a mix. A total with all of them combined is not comparable to a retail new number.

Second, ask about the time period. A month, a quarter and a full year are different, and some months are naturally stronger than others.

Third, put units beside dollars. A rising unit count with falling revenue per unit, or the reverse, tells you something different from either number alone.

Fourth, remember that units are a measure of activity, not of results. They show how busy a dealership was. Whether that activity produced a good return depends on costs, margins and many other things that a unit count cannot show.

## The short version

A unit is one vehicle sold. Dealers split units into new and used, and new units into retail and fleet. National totals are large: more than 16 million light-duty vehicles sold in 2025. But a unit is a count, not a price and not a profit. Learning to ask what is being counted is the first step in reading any dealership report.
