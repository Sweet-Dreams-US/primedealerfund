After a customer agrees on a vehicle and a price, there is usually one more step before the keys change hands. They go to the finance office. This is where the dealership arranges payment and offers a set of optional products. In the industry this area is called finance and insurance, or F&I.

For anyone trying to understand how an auto dealership makes money, F&I is worth knowing. This post explains what the products are, how a dealership gets paid for them, and where the risks sit. It is general education about how dealerships work, not investment advice.

## Where F&I fits in a dealership

A dealership is not one business. It is several departments under one roof. The National Automobile Dealers Association reports sales by department in its annual data report. For 2025, new vehicles made up 54.9% of total dealership sales dollars, used vehicles 31.8%, and service and parts 13.3%.

:::note Worth knowing
The 2025 NADA Data Full-Year Report shows new vehicles at 54.9% of dealership sales dollars, used vehicles at 31.8%, and service and parts at 13.3%. The report measures sales, and it does not break out F&I on its own.
:::

F&I does not appear as its own line in that breakdown, but it sits alongside those departments. It is tied to vehicle sales, because the finance office meets the customer at the moment a new or used vehicle is being purchased.

## The first job: arranging financing

Most customers do not pay for a vehicle in cash. The finance office helps them get a loan or a lease. In its annual report to the SEC, AutoNation, a large public dealer group, says it primarily arranges customer financing through installment loans or leases with third-party lenders, and receives a commission from the lender.

That detail explains the business model. The dealership is usually not the lender. It connects the customer with a lender and is paid a commission for doing so.

## The second job: protection products

The finance office also offers products that protect the customer or the vehicle after the sale. AutoNation's filing lists several: extended service contracts, maintenance programs, guaranteed auto protection (often called GAP), tire and wheel protection, and theft protection.

In plain terms, an extended service contract is meant to help cover certain repair costs after the factory warranty. GAP is meant to cover the difference between what a customer owes on a loan and what the vehicle is worth if it is lost. Maintenance programs are meant to cover scheduled upkeep. Each product has its own terms, and customers should read them before buying.

These products are optional. Customers can ask what each one covers, what it costs, and whether it can be cancelled.

## Who stands behind the products

A dealership does not usually carry the risk of these products itself. AutoNation's filing says its protection products are underwritten and administered by independent third parties, and that it sells them on a commission basis.

There is one more detail. The filing says the company also participates in future underwriting profit on certain products through retrospective commission arrangements with the product issuers. So the dealer's income can include a share of results over time on some products, not only a payment at the time of sale.

:::note Worth knowing
AutoNation's 10-K says its exposure to loss on third-party financing generally is limited to the commissions it receives.
:::

## Why F&I matters to the business

From the dealership's point of view, F&I is income that is attached to a vehicle sale. The finance office meets the customer after the vehicle is chosen, and the commissions described above are earned on top of the sale itself.

It also means F&I depends on vehicle sales volume. When fewer vehicles are sold, there are fewer customers in the finance office. A dealership with strong sales, service, and parts departments is not relying on any one of them.

## Questions a customer can ask

A customer in the finance office can ask a few plain questions about any product. What does it cover, and what does it not? What is the total cost, and how is it paid? Can it be cancelled, and what happens if it is? Who administers it? Clear answers are a sign the dealership understands what it is selling.

## The regulatory side

F&I is regulated, and rules can change. AutoNation's filing warns that regulation from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau or other agencies could lead to significant changes in how dealers are compensated for arranging customer financing, and that this could materially affect its F&I business.

The filing also notes that as its own finance arm grows, income from arranging customer financing is expected to shift toward that arm. This shows that the mix of F&I income is not fixed. A dealer group can change how it earns from financing.

## What to take from this

F&I has two parts: arranging loans and leases, and offering protection products. Dealers are generally paid by commission, with third parties carrying most of the underwriting. It is tied to vehicle sales, and it is shaped by regulation. Anyone looking at a dealership, as a customer or as a student of the business, should treat F&I as one department among several, each with its own risks.
