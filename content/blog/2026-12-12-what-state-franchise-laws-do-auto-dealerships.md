A franchised car dealership does not own its brand. It holds a contract with an automaker that lets it sell that maker's new vehicles, honor its warranties and use its name. That arrangement raises a basic question. What stops the automaker from ending the contract whenever it likes? In the United States, the answer is mostly state law. This article explains what those laws do, in plain terms, and why they matter to anyone studying how dealerships work.

## Why dealers have special protection

An automaker and a dealer are not equals. The automaker is a very large company that controls the product, the supply and the brand. The dealer is usually a much smaller business that has spent heavily on land, a showroom, a service department and staff, all tied to one brand. If the automaker could end the contract on short notice, the dealer would be left with buildings and equipment built for a franchise it no longer holds.

States responded by passing franchise laws for the auto industry. AutoNation, one of the largest public dealership groups in the country, describes these laws in its annual report filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission. It says they were designed to protect dealers and that they make it hard for a manufacturer to terminate or refuse to renew a franchise.

:::note What a public dealer group says
In its 2025 annual report, AutoNation states that under state dealership franchise laws, a manufacturer generally cannot terminate or decline to renew a franchise without "good cause," even if the franchise agreement says otherwise.
:::

## What "good cause" means

The central idea in most of these laws is good cause. An automaker cannot simply decide it wants out. It has to show a legitimate reason, and the law decides what counts.

Franchise agreements themselves list reasons that can support termination or non-renewal. AutoNation notes that these include performance problems such as weak sales volume, poor sales effectiveness and low customer satisfaction, and that these terms are subject to state law. So a dealer that falls well short of reasonable standards can lose a franchise. A dealer that is simply inconvenient, or that the automaker would like to replace, cannot be dropped for that reason alone.

The details differ from state to state. Minnesota's statute is one example. It lists situations that do not count as good cause by themselves, including a change in ownership of the dealership, a dealer's refusal to take goods it did not order, a dealer's other business interests such as selling another make, and a change of location. In some of those cases the automaker's consent is required, but it cannot be unreasonably withheld.

## Who has to prove what

Many of these laws also decide who carries the burden in a dispute. Under the Minnesota statute, the manufacturer has to prove that it acted in good faith, that it followed the notice requirements and that good cause existed. The dealer does not have to prove the termination was wrong. The automaker has to prove it was justified.

That placement of the burden shapes behavior long before any dispute reaches a courtroom. An automaker that knows it will have to document its reasons is more careful about how it ends relationships.

:::note Rules differ by state
The Minnesota statute is one state's version. Other states set their own notice periods, definitions and procedures, so a dealer or investor should look at the law of the state where a store operates.
:::

## Limits on new and relocated stores

Franchise laws do more than limit terminations. They also shape where stores can open. AutoNation explains that state laws restrict a dealer from relocating, or opening another store of the same brand, in an area served by another dealer of that brand. In general the manufacturer must approve a relocation or a new franchise.

This protects an existing dealer from having a nearby competitor of the same brand appear with the automaker's backing. It also means a dealer cannot move freely to a better location without approval. For anyone evaluating a dealership, territory and location rules are part of what the franchise is worth.

## Why franchises are rarely lost

AutoNation reports that dealership franchises are rarely involuntarily terminated or not renewed by the manufacturer outside of bankruptcy. That fits what the laws are meant to do. When termination requires good cause, notice and proof, it becomes difficult and uncommon.

The same report names the exception. If an automaker enters bankruptcy, it could try to end dealer franchises, and the dealer group says it may not receive adequate compensation for them. The report also says that if state franchise laws were repealed or weakened, dealer agreements could become more open to termination, non-renewal or renegotiation. Dealers therefore depend on these laws, and a change in the law is a real business risk.

## What this means for people studying dealerships

Three points are worth keeping. First, a franchise is a contract that state law reinforces, so reading the contract alone does not tell the whole story. Second, the strength of a franchise depends partly on the state where the store sits. Third, these protections are not unlimited. Performance standards, consent rules and bankruptcy all remain.
