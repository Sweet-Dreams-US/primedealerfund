A car dealership sells several different things. It sells new cars, used cars, repair work, parts, and financing and protection products. Each of these earns money in a different way. If a dealership only looked at one total profit number, it would not know which part of the business was doing the work.

That is why dealers report gross profit by department. This article explains what that means, what the published figures show, and what a reader should be careful about. It is general education about the auto retail industry. It is not investment advice, and it is not an offer to sell any security.

## What gross profit is

Gross profit is sales minus the direct cost of what was sold. For a vehicle, that is the selling price minus what the dealer paid for it. For a repair job, it is the revenue from the job minus the direct cost of doing it.

Gross profit is not the same as net profit. A dealership still has to pay salaries, rent, advertising and other costs out of its gross profit. Tracking gross by department is the first step. Tracking costs against each department is the second.

## The departments most dealers track

In its fiscal year 2025 annual report, AutoNation presents four lines: new vehicle, used vehicle, parts and service, and finance and insurance. It says charts in the report show each line's contribution to total revenue and gross profit.

The four reportable segments in that filing are different. They are Domestic, Import, Premium Luxury and AutoNation Finance. Departments describe what is being sold. Segments describe how a company groups its stores and businesses for reporting. Both can appear in the same report, so a reader needs to know which one a number refers to.

The fixed operations department is another term worth knowing. Mercer Capital, writing about NADA data, uses it for the service, parts and body shop departments together.

## What the numbers show

The National Automobile Dealers Association, or NADA, publishes financial profiles of an average dealership. Mercer Capital reports these figures for fixed operations from those profiles:

- Gross margin on fixed operations sales was 46.6% in 2019, 47.0% in 2020 and 47.2% in the first ten months of 2021.
- Fixed operations were 12.4% of total dealership sales in 2019, 12.0% in 2020 and 10.9% in the first ten months of 2021.
- Fixed operations were 50.5% of total dealership gross profit in 2019, 46.1% in 2020 and 36.5% in the first ten months of 2021.

For vehicle sales, the same article says gross profit margins on new and used vehicles were 5.5% and 11.3% in 2019.

:::note Worth knowing
In 2019, fixed operations made up 12.4% of the average dealership's sales and 50.5% of its gross profit, according to the NADA figures reported by Mercer Capital. The department that is small in sales can be large in gross profit.
:::

This is the main reason to track by department. The department with the most sales is not always the one that produces the most gross profit. A total sales figure would hide that.

The 2021 figure for fixed operations is lower than the 2019 and 2020 figures. The source covers only the first ten months of 2021, and it shows both a rise in the margin and a fall in the share of gross profit. A reader should not assume a cause that the source does not state. It is enough to see that the department mix changed from year to year.

## Why dealers do it

Deloitte Australia's 2025 dealership benchmarks give a clear description of the purpose. The booklet is based on Australian dealer data, so it is not a U.S. measure, but its reasoning applies to the idea of department tracking in general. It frames the work around two questions: where the gross comes from, and how strong the margins are.

From that, department tracking supports four uses:

- It shows the revenue mix. The benchmarks split gross profit between the front end, which is vehicle sales, and the back end, which is parts, service and F&I. The booklet notes that businesses that deliver sustainable results over a longer time frame tend to have a slight front-end bias.
- It shows margin strength. Each department has its own target, so a weak result can be traced to a specific area, such as used vehicle margins or service gross.
- It controls costs. Costs such as sales salaries, floorplan interest and advertising can be measured against the gross of the department they belong to.
- It supports comparison. A dealer can compare itself with peers in the same market category and adjust its focus.

## What to be careful about

Three cautions apply to anyone reading department figures.

First, check the year and the country. The NADA figures above come from the United States and cover 2019 to 2021. The Deloitte data is from Australia and covers 2025. They are not interchangeable.

Second, check whether a figure is a margin, a share or a dollar amount. A department can have a high margin and a small share of total gross profit, or the reverse.

Third, remember that averages describe an average dealership. An individual store can look quite different, depending on its brand, its market and how it runs each department.

## Summary

Dealers track gross profit by department to see where their gross comes from, how strong each margin is, and where costs are growing. The published NADA figures show that a department can be small in sales and large in gross profit. When you read any dealership's numbers, confirm which department, which year and which market the figure describes.
