When you look at a car dealership, the most visible asset is the row of vehicles on the lot. That stock is called inventory, and it is the product the dealership sells. It is also one of the largest sums of money a dealership has to find before it sells anything. Understanding how that inventory is paid for is one of the first steps in understanding how a dealership business works.

## What vehicle inventory is

Inventory is every vehicle a dealership owns and holds for sale. For a franchised new-car dealer, that means new vehicles bought from the manufacturer, plus used vehicles taken in on trade or bought at auction. Each vehicle sits on the books as an asset until it is sold, and each one has a purchase price the dealer has already paid.

The scale of the industry shows why this matters. The National Automobile Dealers Association (NADA) reports that in calendar year 2025 the nation's 16,990 franchised light-vehicle dealers sold 16.2 million light-duty vehicles, and that total sales topped $1.3 trillion. Every one of those vehicles had to be bought by a dealer first, usually well before a customer walked in.

## Why dealers do not pay for inventory with cash

A dealer cannot realistically pay cash for hundreds of vehicles at once and wait for them to sell. The money would be tied up on the lot for weeks or months. Instead, dealers buy their stock with a specific kind of loan. NADA explains that local dealerships purchase their inventories through financing called "floor plan lending."

The name comes from the idea of financing the cars on the showroom floor and lot. The loan exists so the dealer can have a full selection to show customers without holding that much cash in reserve.

## How floor plan lending works

A floor plan loan is secured by the vehicles themselves. NADA describes it as financing through a lender that is secured by each vehicle and its VIN number. The VIN, the vehicle identification number, is how a lender identifies a particular car as collateral for a particular amount of debt.

The loans are often set up as a budget. NADA says they are based on an aggregate budget, and gives an example of a dealer who might be able to borrow $10 million over the year to purchase 300 new cars. As vehicles arrive, the manufacturer is paid the invoice price, and NADA notes that payment is usually drafted directly from the dealer's bank account.

NADA also says the loans are often made with a one year term. A one year term does not mean a dealer stops needing the financing after a year. Dealerships buy and sell vehicles continuously, so the financing is an ongoing part of running the business.

## Who lends the money

Floor plan lenders come in several types. NADA lists local and regional banks, large national banks, and financing companies. It also points to the finance companies owned by manufacturers, such as Toyota Financial or Ford Credit.

Because the loan is secured by inventory, lenders protect themselves by checking it. NADA says the loans are inspected periodically by lenders, who check the inventory of all collateralized vehicles. The collateral can reach beyond the cars. NADA says dealership collateral can include buildings and property, and that loans are often guaranteed by the dealer personally.

## Why interest rates matter so much

Because the dealer borrows to hold inventory, the cost of that borrowing is a real operating cost. NADA states that a one percent rate increase can raise some dealership costs by millions of dollars. It also says the ability to deduct interest expenses is critical for the business model to work.

This is the reason investors who study dealerships pay attention to floor plan costs. A dealership with a lot full of slow-moving vehicles pays interest on those vehicles every day they are unsold. A dealership that turns its inventory quickly carries that cost for a shorter time on each vehicle.

## What to take away

Inventory is both the product a dealership sells and a loan it carries. The key points are simple. Dealers finance their vehicles through floor plan lending. Each loan is secured by the vehicles and tracked by VIN. Lenders include banks and manufacturer finance companies. And because the debt is tied to every unsold car, interest rates and inventory turnover shape a dealership's costs.

If you are an investor reading about a dealership business, a good habit is to ask three questions. How is the inventory financed? What happens to costs if rates change? How quickly does the stock sell? The answers tell you a great deal about the health of the operation.
