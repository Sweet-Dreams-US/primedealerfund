When most people think about a car dealership, they think about the showroom. People who study dealerships as businesses often start somewhere else: the service department. This article explains why, using national figures from the National Automobile Dealers Association (NADA). It is general education about the auto retail industry, not investment advice.

## The department behind the showroom

A franchised dealership does more than sell vehicles. It also repairs and maintains them, and it sells the parts used for that work. These are usually handled in the service and parts departments, which are separate from the sales floor.

A customer comes to the showroom when they decide to buy a vehicle. A customer comes to the service department when something needs maintenance or repair. The two visits happen for different reasons and at different times, which is part of why analysts look at them separately.

## What the national numbers show

NADA publishes an annual report on the dealer industry. Its 2025 full-year report says the nation's 16,990 franchised light-vehicle dealers sold 16.2 million light-duty vehicles, and that total franchised light-vehicle dealership sales topped $1.3 trillion.

The same report says dealerships wrote more than 276 million repair orders, with service and parts sales exceeding $164 billion.

:::note Worth knowing
A repair order is the record a dealership writes when it does service work on a vehicle. NADA reports more than 276 million of them in 2025 across the country's franchised light-vehicle dealers.
:::

To put those figures side by side, $164 billion is roughly 12 to 13 percent of $1.3 trillion. That division is ours, not NADA's, and it compares sales only. It does not tell you how much profit each department earns. The NADA summary page does not include a breakdown of profit by department. That breakdown would be in the full report, which readers can download from NADA.

## Sales are not profit

This point matters, so it is worth slowing down. Revenue, also called sales, is the money that comes in. Profit is what is left after the costs of earning that money. A department with a smaller share of sales can have a larger or smaller share of profit than its share of sales suggests, because the costs differ from one department to another.

For that reason, a careful reader does not stop at a sales figure. If you see a claim that one department is the most important to a dealership, look for the profit figure and the year it covers. If the figure is not shown, treat the claim as incomplete.

## Why service gets so much attention

There are a few reasons people who study dealerships look closely at service. These are general observations, not findings from the NADA summary.

First, service is a different kind of business from vehicle sales. A vehicle sale happens when a customer chooses to buy. Service can happen many times over the life of a vehicle that has already been sold. So a count of repair orders gives a different view of customer activity than a count of vehicles sold.

Second, service ties a dealership to customers after the sale. A customer who returns for maintenance gives the dealership another chance to build a relationship.

Third, service and sales do not always move together. How each department performs in a slow year for vehicle sales is a fair question for any reader to ask, and the answer should come from data for that dealership, not from a general rule.

## What a company says versus what the data shows

Companies that own dealerships often describe their service departments in positive terms. That is normal. It is also why it helps to know where the numbers come from.

## Questions worth asking about any dealership

Whether you are reading about a fund, a single dealership, or the industry as a whole, a few simple questions help. What share of sales comes from each department? What share of profit comes from each department? What year does the figure cover? Who published it, and where can it be checked? What does the source say it cannot show?

NADA's own page is a good example of the last point. It reports national totals for 2025 and points readers to its full report for more detail. A source that tells you what it does not cover is easier to trust than one that does not.

## Where to read further

You can read the NADA summary yourself on the NADA website under NADA Data. If you want department-level detail, the full report is the place to look. Check the year on any figure you use, because these numbers change from year to year.
