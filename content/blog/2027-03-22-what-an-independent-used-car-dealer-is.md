People who invest in automotive retail, or who just follow it, often use the word "dealer" as if it meant one thing. It does not. An independent used car dealer and a franchised dealer run different businesses, with different rules, different income sources and different risks. This post explains what an independent dealer is and how it differs from a franchised store.

The comparison draws mainly on a 2024 valuation article from Mercer Capital, a business valuation firm, and on consumer guidance from the Federal Trade Commission. It is general education about how the industry is organized.

## The basic definition

A franchised dealer has a formal agreement with a vehicle manufacturer. A Nissan store or a Chevrolet store sells new vehicles of that brand under that agreement. An independent dealer has no such agreement. As Mercer Capital puts it, independent dealers do not have formal agreements with the vehicle manufacturer.

That one difference shapes almost everything else. An independent dealer does not retail new vehicles. It buys used vehicles and sells them to customers.

Mercer Capital notes that independent dealers also lack area protection, and that lower barriers to entry mean more competition. A franchised dealer's agreement with a manufacturer is tied to a place. An independent can open down the road from another independent with no manufacturer involved.

:::note Worth knowing
Mercer Capital estimates 27,000 to 30,000 independent dealers in the United States, counting individuals who file as used car dealers. It puts physical independent and used car dealerships at 16,000 to 17,000.
:::

## How the income differs

A franchised dealer typically runs four profit centers: new vehicles, used vehicles, service and parts, and finance and insurance. Mercer Capital says independents usually run only some of these, and do not sell new vehicles.

That means an independent lot depends more heavily on used vehicle sales, and on the margin between what it pays for a car and what it sells it for. The owner's skill at sourcing and pricing cars matters a great deal. Mercer Capital observes that personal goodwill is generally more likely with independents, though not guaranteed, because those skills drive profit.

## How the dealers pay for inventory

Dealers usually finance their inventory with a floor plan, which is a credit line secured by the cars on the lot. Mercer Capital says franchised dealers typically use manufacturer finance arms, such as Ford Motor Credit or Toyota Credit. Independents tend to use regional banks and firms like NextGear Capital, and their floor plan limits are typically lower.

## Who the customers are

According to Mercer Capital, independents serve lower and middle-income buyers. As a result, they feel recessions, unemployment and high interest rates more than franchised dealers do. Collections matter more for independents too, especially for Buy Here Pay Here dealers, where the dealer finances the sale itself. Repossessions are a routine cost of doing business for those dealers, while they are typically one-off events for franchised dealers.

## Reporting and the used car market

Franchised dealers report financials to their manufacturers monthly, so their data is more uniform and easier to find. Independent dealer data is harder to come by. Mercer Capital says independents lack published valuation multiples, which makes comparing them to other businesses harder. For anyone trying to understand the sector from the outside, this is a real difference. There is simply less public information about independents.

Independents and franchised stores both sell used vehicles. Mercer Capital cites a Cox Automotive projection of 36.2 million used unit sales in 2024, up from 35.9 million in 2023, with retail used units projected at 19.2 million. Those figures cover the whole used car market, not independents alone, and they are a 2024 projection.

## What the law asks of any used car dealer

Some rules do not depend on which kind of dealer sells the car. The FTC says dealers must display a Buyers Guide on every used car they offer and give it to the buyer after the sale. The guide shows whether the car is sold as is or with a warranty, and what share of repair costs the dealer pays under any warranty. The FTC adds that buyers are entitled to a Buyers Guide when buying a used car online.

The FTC page speaks of dealers in general and does not separate independent from franchised stores, so this is a baseline for both.

:::note Worth knowing
Per the FTC, the Buyers Guide overrides conflicting terms in the sales contract. Any warranty change you negotiate should appear on both documents.
:::

In short, an independent used car dealer has no formal agreement with a manufacturer, sells used vehicles, has no area protection, and usually relies on fewer profit centers than a franchised store. It serves a different set of customers, finances its inventory differently, and is harder to study because less of its data is public. A franchised dealer holds a manufacturer agreement and typically runs new vehicles, used vehicles, service and parts, and finance and insurance. Both must follow the same federal Buyers Guide rule on used cars.
