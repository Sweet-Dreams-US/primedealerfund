Car dealerships fall into two broad groups. Franchised dealers sell cars under an agreement with a manufacturer. Independent dealers do not have that tie. Customers often ask which kind is better, but a more useful question is what actually changes for the buyer and what stays the same. This article compares the two using the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), CarGurus, and Wikipedia.

## What makes a dealer franchised

Wikipedia explains that dealers sell new or used cars, "typically under a franchise agreement with an automaker or its authorized distributor." Dealerships are usually franchised to sell and service vehicles for specific companies. Under franchise laws, new cars must be sold by licensed and bonded, independently owned dealerships.

CarGurus describes the result from a shopper's side. Franchise dealerships are the main option for new vehicles, but they also move a significant amount of used cars. A franchised dealer is tied to one manufacturer's brand. Independent dealers, in contrast, specialize in used car sales and have no affiliation with a car manufacturer.

## What changes: new cars and certified pre-owned

The first change is what is on the lot. If you want a new car from a particular brand, a franchised dealer for that brand is the main place to find one, according to CarGurus.

The second change is certified pre-owned (CPO) vehicles. CarGurus says franchised dealerships also offer CPO vehicles, and that CPO cars are typically backed with their own warranties. It recommends factory-certified cars, which meet standards set by the original manufacturer that are generally more thorough than dealership CPOs. It also says independent dealers will not be able to provide manufacturer CPO cars.

:::note Certified pre-owned
CarGurus says manufacturer CPO cars are available through franchised dealerships, and independent dealers cannot provide them. Standards for a dealership's own CPO label can be different from the manufacturer's.
:::

## What changes: service, price, and financing

Service is another difference. CarGurus says the main advantage of a franchised dealer is the connection to the manufacturer, which can pay dividends for servicing vehicles. In-house mechanics know the automaker's cars well, and parts are easy to get directly from the manufacturer. Wikipedia adds a business point: dealership profits in the US mainly come from servicing, some from used cars, and little from new cars.

Price and negotiation can differ too. CarGurus says buyers at a franchised dealer can easily pay more than they would elsewhere, with taxes and fees on top, and that the dealer may add extras to close a sale. It describes independent dealers as a good option for price-conscious shoppers, with negotiation that is often more intense, though some larger chains advertise no-haggle pricing.

Financing is a third area. CarGurus says a franchised dealer's finance arm may be risk-averse and not willing to set up a monthly payment plan for buyers with poor or unestablished credit. It also advises researching financing at banks and credit unions first.

Wikipedia notes a broader debate about the franchise system. It reports that the arrangement can raise costs for consumers, and that economists argue regulations protecting dealerships increase car costs. These are general claims about the system, not a statement about any single dealer.

## What stays the same: the FTC rules for used cars

Whichever kind of dealer you choose, the FTC's consumer guidance applies when you buy a used car from a dealer. Several points do not depend on the dealer's type.

A Buyers Guide must be on every used car. The FTC says dealers have to put a Buyers Guide on every used car, and that you are entitled to one when buying online. The Guide shows whether the car is sold "as is" or with a warranty, and what share of repair costs the dealer will cover. The FTC says it overrides anything in your sales contract.

As is means you carry the risk. In an "as is" sale the dealer will not pay for repairs after the sale. The FTC explains that you are assuming the risk of anything that goes wrong after the sale. Implied warranties apply to cars without a written warranty, unless the sale is "as is," and can last as long as four years depending on your state.

Promises belong in writing. The FTC says spoken promises are difficult to enforce.

An inspection is still your job. The FTC states that a vehicle history report is not a substitute for an independent vehicle inspection, and advises paying an independent mechanic to inspect the car, even a certified one. If a dealer refuses an independent inspection, the FTC says to consider going elsewhere.

:::note From the FTC
Federal law does not require dealers to give you three days to cancel the deal and return the car. Some states require a cancellation right, and elsewhere it exists only if the dealer offers it.
:::

## What stays the same: add-ons and financing math

The FTC warns that add-ons such as gap insurance or rustproofing can cost thousands, and that you can usually buy the vehicle you want without any add-ons. It suggests asking what each add-on costs and what it includes, and getting the answers in writing.

On financing, the FTC says low monthly payment offers may be tempting but often have longer loan periods and higher interest, making them more costly overall. It advises comparing the total cost, including the finance charge and APR, not just the monthly payment. It also says a service contract, sometimes called an extended warranty, is not a warranty.

These points apply at a franchised store and an independent lot alike. Nothing about the dealer's type changes the need to read the paperwork.

## A simple way to compare

Putting the sources together, a buyer can sort the choice into a few questions. Do you want a new car, or a manufacturer-certified used car? Then a franchised dealer for that brand is the place CarGurus points to. Are you shopping mainly on price for a used car? Then CarGurus describes independent dealers as an option worth a look. In both cases the FTC's steps stay the same: read the Buyers Guide, get promises in writing, have an independent mechanic inspect the car, and compare the total cost of any financing.
