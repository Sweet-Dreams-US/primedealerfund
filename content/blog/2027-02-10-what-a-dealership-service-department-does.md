Most people picture a car dealership as a place where vehicles are sold. Behind the showroom is a second business that runs all week long: the service department. It repairs and maintains vehicles, and it handles work that only a franchised dealer is allowed to do. This article explains what that department does, how it gets paid, and why people who study dealerships pay close attention to it.

## What the department is for

A service department performs repair and maintenance work on vehicles. That includes routine maintenance and larger repairs. In its annual report, AutoNation describes its parts and service business as "automotive repair and maintenance services as well as wholesale parts and collision businesses." Dealerships often sell parts to other businesses as well as using them in their own shop.

The department matters because it keeps a relationship going after the sale. A buyer drives a vehicle home once, but the same vehicle needs attention again and again over the years it is on the road. Each of those visits is a chance for the dealer to earn revenue without selling another car.

## Three kinds of work

The work that comes through the service drive falls into a few groups.

Customer-pay work is the ordinary repair and maintenance that the vehicle owner pays for directly. An oil change, a brake job, or a tire rotation are common examples.

Warranty work is repair covered by the manufacturer's warranty. The owner does not pay for it. The dealer performs the repair and bills the manufacturer. AutoNation notes in its filing that it has "significant receivables from manufacturers for warranty and service work performed for customers," which shows how large this billing can become.

Recall work is repair ordered by the manufacturer to fix a known problem. AutoNation says its stores perform "manufacturer recall repairs and other warranty work that can be performed only at franchised dealerships and customer-pay service work." That wording matters. Recall and warranty repairs can only be done at a franchised dealer, so independent shops cannot take that work.

:::note Worth knowing
AutoNation states that manufacturer recall repairs and other warranty work "can be performed only at franchised dealerships." Customer-pay work, by contrast, can also go to other shops.
:::

## How a visit moves through the shop

A visit starts when a customer arrives and describes a problem or asks for maintenance. The department writes up a repair order, which is the record of the work requested and later performed. A technician does the work, and the shop uses parts from the dealership's own parts inventory.

The National Automobile Dealers Association (NADA) tracks this activity across the industry. In its 2025 full-year report, dealerships wrote 276,128,228 repair orders in total, or 16,252 per dealership. That is the scale of the traffic moving through these departments in a year.

People are a large part of the picture too. The same report counts 278,424 technicians, including body shop technicians, which averages 16 per dealership. Parts take up space and money as well. NADA reports total parts inventory of $9,724,771,613 across dealerships, or $572,382 per dealership.

## How the department earns money

A service department sells two things together: labor and parts. NADA reports that parts sales per service labor sale were $1.43 in 2025, so on average a dealership sold about $1.43 of parts for each $1.00 of service labor.

The size of each repair order differs by who is paying. NADA reports service and parts sales of $494 per customer repair order and $551 per warranty repair order. The average customer mechanical labor rate was $186.

These are industry averages. A single dealership's numbers depend on its brand, its location, its staff, and the vehicles it serves.

## Where service and parts fit in the dealership

NADA reports that service and parts made up 13.3% of total dealership sales dollars in 2025, compared with 13.2% in 2024. New vehicles were 54.9% and used vehicles were 31.8%. Total service and parts sales for all dealerships were $164.6 billion, or $9,687,942 per dealership.

Sales dollars are not the same as profit, and NADA's report in this section does not break out gross profit. Still, AutoNation describes its parts and service business as "higher-margin" and says it "has historically been less sensitive to macroeconomic conditions." In plain terms, the company says people keep repairing their vehicles even when they are less eager to buy new ones.

## Questions that help you understand any service department

If you are studying how dealerships work, a few questions show how a service department is doing. How many repair orders does it write? What share is customer-pay, and what share is warranty? How many technicians does it have, and is the shop able to keep them busy? How much parts inventory does it carry compared with the work it does?

None of these questions has a single right answer. They help a reader see what the department actually does and how it fits with the new and used vehicle sides of the business.

## The short version

A franchised dealer's service department repairs and maintains vehicles, handles warranty and recall work that only franchised dealers can do, and sells parts and labor together. Across the industry, NADA's numbers show a very large volume of repair orders each year. Service and parts are a smaller share of sales dollars than new and used vehicles, yet AutoNation calls the business higher-margin and less sensitive to the economy.
