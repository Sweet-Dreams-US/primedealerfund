When you shop for a new car, you choose from what is on the lot. A dealer is in a different position. The new vehicles on that lot came from a manufacturer, and the rules for how they got there were set long before any shopper walked in. This post explains the new vehicle supply path in plain terms, using a public company filing and trade association data.

The filing used here comes from AutoNation, one of the large public dealer groups. It describes one company, so treat it as a clear example of how the new vehicle side of the business is set up, not as a description of every dealership.

## It starts with a franchise agreement

A new vehicle store sells one manufacturer's brand under a contract. AutoNation states in its fiscal 2025 annual report that it operates "each of our new vehicle stores under a franchise agreement with a vehicle manufacturer or distributor."

The filing describes the right granted by that contract as "a non-exclusive right to sell the manufacturer's or distributor's brand of vehicles." In other words, the manufacturer decides who is allowed to sell its brand, and the dealer sells under the terms of the agreement.

The manufacturer's influence does not stop at the contract. The same filing says that vehicle manufacturers and distributors with whom the company holds franchises have "significant influence over the operations" of its stores. It also says manufacturers "impose minimum facility requirements that can require significant capital expenditures." A dealer that wants to keep a franchise has to meet those standards.

## Where the vehicles come from

The filing says the company acquires new vehicles for retail sale "either directly from the applicable automotive manufacturer" or "through dealer trades with other stores of the same brand franchise."

That gives two routes. The first is a purchase from the automaker. The second is a trade between stores that sell the same brand, which lets one store pass vehicles to another that has a buyer for them.

The filing is also direct about how much the company depends on the first route. It says "we rely exclusively on the various vehicle manufacturers for our new vehicle inventory." A new vehicle store does not stock its brand from just anywhere. Its supply runs through the manufacturer and, for trades, through other stores of the same brand.

## Allocation: who decides what arrives

Among the risks it lists, AutoNation names the manufacturer's ability to "allocate to our stores an attractive, high-quality, and desirable product mix." The word allocate is worth noticing. The filing treats the mix of vehicles a store receives as something that depends on the manufacturer, not only on what the store wants to sell.

The filing does not describe the allocation process in detail, so this post does not either. What it does show is that a store's inventory is shaped by two things at once: what the store orders and what the manufacturer supplies.

:::note Worth knowing
AutoNation's 2025 annual report states that it relies "exclusively on the various vehicle manufacturers for our new vehicle inventory." That is the company's own description of its supply.
:::

## Incentives and timing

Manufacturers also try to move vehicles with incentive programs. The filing says "most vehicle manufacturers from time to time establish various marketing and sales incentive programs," and that manufacturers provide "direct financial assistance in various areas."

There is a planning problem built into this. The filing notes that incentive programs are often not announced in advance, which makes them "difficult to plan for when ordering inventory." A dealer deciding how many vehicles to order has to do so without knowing exactly what support will be offered later.

## The cost of holding inventory

Vehicles on a lot have a carrying cost. The filing calls one part of it "new vehicle floorplan interest expense net of floorplan assistance that we receive from automotive manufacturers." The company says this expense can rise with interest rates, with inventory levels, or with lower manufacturer assistance.

That is why inventory levels matter to a dealer's results. The National Automobile Dealers Association (NADA) publishes a full-year data report that gives a picture of the industry. For 2025 it reports new vehicle days' supply of 47 for domestic brands and 41 for import brands. It also reports 2,577,974 new vehicles in inventory, counting vehicles on the ground and in transit. Days' supply is a way of comparing inventory with the pace of sales.

## How big the new vehicle business is

NADA's 2025 report gives some scale. It counts 16,990 new light-vehicle dealerships. It reports that the average dealership sold 955 new vehicles, and that total light-duty vehicle sales were 16,233,363, or about 16.2 million.

New vehicles are also the largest piece of dealership sales dollars. The report puts the share of total dealership sales dollars from new vehicles at 54.9 percent, compared with 54.7 percent the year before. That figure is about sales dollars. It does not say how much of a dealership's profit comes from new vehicles, and the report does not give that number.

:::note Worth knowing
NADA's 2025 data report lists 16,990 new light-vehicle dealerships and says new vehicles made up 54.9 percent of total dealership sales dollars.
:::

## Questions to ask when you read about a dealership

If you read about a dealership group, a few questions follow from the supply path above.

First, which franchises does it hold, and what does each agreement require of the stores? Second, how does it get its inventory, and how does it pay for the vehicles it holds? Third, how much of its revenue comes from new vehicles compared with other parts of the business?

The answers to these questions explain a lot about how a dealership behaves from month to month. They also explain why dealers pay attention to manufacturer incentives, inventory levels and interest rates.
