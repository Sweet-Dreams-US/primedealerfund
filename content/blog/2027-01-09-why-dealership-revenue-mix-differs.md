A car dealership earns money in four main ways: selling new vehicles, selling used vehicles, arranging finance and insurance, and running service and parts. Almost every franchised dealer in the country has all four. Yet if you compared the books of two dealerships, the share of income from each stream could look very different.

This post explains why. For anyone learning how auto retail works, including people who study the sector as investors, the mix matters because it shapes how stable a dealership's income is and where its attention goes each day.

## The four streams, briefly

New vehicle sales bring in the most dollars of sales at most dealerships, but not always the most profit per dollar. Used vehicle sales add a second source of inventory and customers. Finance and insurance, often called F&I, is the work of arranging loans, leases, and products at the time of sale. Service and parts is the repair and maintenance side, sometimes called fixed operations, and some dealerships also run a body shop.

All four are connected. A new vehicle sale can lead to a trade in that is sold as a used vehicle, a loan that is handled in F&I, and years of service visits. The mix describes how much each part contributes.

## The national picture is one average

The National Automobile Dealers Association publishes yearly data on its member dealerships. In its 2025 full-year report, new vehicles made up 54.9 percent of total dealership sales dollars, used vehicles made up 31.8 percent, and service and parts made up 13.3 percent. The report shows these shares barely moved from 2024.

:::note National averages
In the 2025 NADA Data report, new vehicles were 54.9 percent of dealership sales dollars, used vehicles were 31.8 percent, and service and parts were 13.3 percent. The report does not break out F&I in these shares.
:::

That is an average across thousands of stores. A real dealership will sit above or below each of these numbers. The next sections cover the main reasons.

## Brand changes the mix

A dealership sells the brands it holds a franchise for, and brands differ in price, in how many vehicles they sell, and in the kind of buyer they attract. A luxury brand has a higher price on each vehicle. A volume brand sells more units at lower prices. A truck focused brand may do well with certain customers and services.

AutoNation, a large public dealer group, shows this in its annual report for 2025. It reports that about 37 percent of its revenue came from premium luxury franchises, about 30 percent from import brands, and about 27 percent from domestic brands. Its new vehicle revenue table shows that import brands sold the most units, while premium luxury brands brought in the most new vehicle revenue. Units and revenue do not always rank the same way.

## Location changes the mix

Where a dealership sits affects who walks in. A store in a large metro area may sell more luxury vehicles and have more competition from nearby stores. A rural store may sell more trucks, may rely more on used vehicles, and may have loyal service customers who live close and have few other places to go.

Local conditions matter too: weather, local incomes, the age of the vehicles already on the road, and how many rival dealers share the same area. AutoNation's report shows how concentrated a single group can be by place. It lists Florida, Texas, and California as about 65 percent of its total revenue. A group that is spread across several smaller markets would show a different pattern.

## Size changes the mix

Larger dealerships often have more room for used inventory, more service bays, and more staff for F&I. A small store may have a strong new vehicle franchise and a smaller service department. Size also changes how much of the business depends on a few large months or a few key employees.

For service and parts, the NADA report gives one useful measure. Total service and parts sales for 2025 were about 164.6 billion dollars, or roughly 9.7 million dollars per dealership on average. A dealership well above or below that figure is running a service business of a different scale than the typical store.

## Why a mix is neither good nor bad on its own

It is tempting to say a dealership with more service income is safer, or one with more used sales is riskier. The mix by itself does not say that. What matters is how a stream is run and how it holds up when conditions change.

Some questions help when studying a dealership:

- Does the store depend on one stream for most of its income?
- Which brands does it hold, and how strong is each brand's lineup?
- How much of the service business comes from repeat customers?
- How does the store do when new vehicle sales slow down?

New vehicle sales move with interest rates, supply, and consumer confidence. Service and parts tend to be less tied to the sales cycle because vehicles on the road need repair either way. These are general patterns, and they differ by store.

## Reading a dealership's numbers

When you look at a dealership or a dealer group, start with the revenue by stream and compare it to the national average. Then ask what in its brands, location, and size explains the gap. Where the answer is not clear, that is a question to take to the people who run the store.
