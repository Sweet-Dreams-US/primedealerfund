People now buy used cars in two main ways. They can walk onto a dealer's lot, or they can shop on a website and have the car delivered or picked up. The channels feel different, but the buyer's questions are much the same. What is the real price, what happens if something breaks, and what do I have in writing? This post compares the two channels using guidance from the Federal Trade Commission, and gives you a checklist you can use with either one.

## The two channels in plain terms

A dealership sells vehicles from a physical location. You can see the car, drive it and talk to a salesperson in person. An online used car retailer sells through a website. You choose a car from photos and a listing, and the car comes to you or you pick it up.

Neither channel is automatically better. What matters is whether you can check the car, understand the terms and get every promise in writing. The sections below go through the items the FTC highlights for buying from a dealer, and apply them to both.

## The Buyers Guide

The FTC says dealers have to display a Buyers Guide in every used car they offer for sale, and they also have to give it to buyers after the sale. The FTC adds that you are also entitled to a Buyers Guide if you buy a used car online.

:::note Worth knowing
According to the Federal Trade Commission, a Buyers Guide is required on every used car a dealer offers, and buyers are entitled to one when they buy a used car online. The Guide overrides your sales contract, so changes you negotiate should appear in both.
:::

So the first step in either channel is to ask to see the Buyers Guide before you commit. It tells you whether the car comes with a dealer warranty or is sold as is. If you buy online and the listing does not show it, ask for it.

## Warranties and as-is sales

The FTC explains that a sale marked "As Is" with no dealer warranty means the dealer will not pay for repairs, and you take on the risk of anything that goes wrong after the sale. If the car has a warranty, the FTC advises getting a copy and reading what it covers, because most used car warranties are limited and the coverage varies.

The FTC also says implied warranties still apply to a used car without a written warranty, unless the car is sold as is. It notes that implied warranty coverage can last as long as four years, depending on the state.

For a manufacturer warranty, the FTC says to verify coverage with the dealership using the vehicle identification number.

Apply this to both channels the same way:

1. Find out whether the car is sold as is or with a warranty.
2. Get any warranty in writing and read it.
3. Ask who pays for repairs in the first weeks and months.
4. Check the manufacturer warranty by VIN, if one is claimed.

## Promises must be in writing

The FTC warns that spoken promises are difficult to enforce. If a salesperson tells you a repair will be done or a part will be replaced, ask for that promise on the Buyers Guide and in the contract. For an as-is sale, the FTC says any verbal promises, such as promised repairs, should be written on the Buyers Guide.

This matters even more online, where a chat message or a phone call may be the only record of what was said. Ask for the promise in a document, not only in a message.

## Inspections and history reports

The FTC states that a vehicle history report is not a substitute for an independent vehicle inspection, because history reports typically do not list mechanical problems. It recommends a mechanical inspection even if the dealer has certified the car. It also suggests asking the mechanic for a written report with repair cost estimates, which you can use to negotiate the price.

:::note Worth knowing
The FTC says a vehicle history report does not replace an independent inspection, and that if a dealer will not allow an independent inspection, you should consider another dealer.
:::

At a dealership, you can often ask to take the car to a mechanic. With an online retailer, ask before you buy how an independent inspection can be arranged, and what the terms are if you decide against the car after it arrives. Terms differ between sellers, so read them rather than assuming.

## Financing and the total price

The FTC says financing increases the total cost of the car, because you also pay interest and other credit costs. It adds that low monthly payments are often tied to longer loan terms and higher interest rates, which makes the total cost higher.

Before you sign, the FTC says to confirm the exact car price, the total sales price with financing, the finance charge, the APR, and the number of payments and their amounts. Ask for these numbers from any seller, in person or online, and compare them side by side.

## A checklist for either channel

Use this list when you compare a dealer and an online retailer for the same kind of car:

1. Ask to see the Buyers Guide.
2. Confirm whether the car is sold as is or with a warranty.
3. Get every promise in writing.
4. Arrange an independent mechanical inspection and ask for a written report.
5. Read a vehicle history report, and remember it does not replace the inspection.
6. Confirm the price, total price with financing, finance charge, APR and payment schedule.
7. Read the seller's terms before you pay anything.

If a seller will not let you do these things, the FTC's own suggestion on inspections is to consider another seller.
