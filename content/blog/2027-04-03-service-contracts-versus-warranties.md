People often use the words warranty and service contract as if they meant the same thing. They do not. The difference affects what a car buyer is promised, what a dealership sells, and how the federal government expects dealers to disclose it. This article explains both terms using guidance from the Federal Trade Commission, and it is part of a series that introduces how auto retail works.

## Why the difference comes up

A dealership earns money from several parts of its business. The site of Prime Dealer Fund lists new and used vehicle sales, finance and insurance, service, parts, and body shop work at some locations as the revenue streams of an automotive dealership. Finance and insurance, often shortened to F&I, is the department where a buyer arranges financing and may be offered add-on products. Service contracts are one of those products.

Because buyers meet these terms at the point of sale, the rules around them are written to protect buyers from confusion. Understanding the rules helps anyone who wants to know how a dealership works, whether as a customer or as someone studying the industry.

## What a warranty is

A warranty is a promise from the dealer or manufacturer about the condition of a vehicle. The FTC says warranties can be full or limited, and that "Most used car warranties are limited and their coverage varies." A limited warranty covers only what the paperwork says it covers, for as long as it says.

A written warranty is not the only kind. The FTC explains that implied warranties still apply to a used car without a written warranty, unless the car is sold "as is." Implied warranties come from state law, and the FTC says they "can last as long as four years," depending on the state.

:::note Worth knowing
The FTC says a used car sold with no written warranty still carries implied warranties unless it is sold "as is," and that their length depends on state law.
:::

## What "as is" means

When a used car is sold "as is," the dealer is not promising to pay for repairs after the sale. The FTC puts it this way: "You're assuming the risk of anything that goes wrong after the sale." State law may limit "as is" sales in some places, and the FTC notes that some states require different wording on the guide that dealers post.

The FTC also warns that verbal promises can be hard to enforce. A promise made at the sales desk should be written on the Buyers Guide, because otherwise "you may have a hard time getting the dealer to follow through."

## What a service contract is

The FTC states that "a service contract is not a warranty." A service contract is a separate agreement, usually sold for an added price, in which a company promises to perform or pay for certain repairs or services. The company may be the dealer or a third party, and the terms are set by the contract, not by state implied warranty law.

That means two cars with the same sticker price can come with very different protection. One may have a limited warranty and no contract. Another may be "as is" with a service contract the buyer paid extra for. The paperwork, not the name on it, tells you what is covered.

## How the Buyers Guide shows the difference

Federal rules make dealers display a Buyers Guide in every used car they offer. The FTC says a dealer must post it "before you display a vehicle for sale," that it must be prominent with both sides visible, and that the buyer must receive the original or a copy at the sale, reflecting all final changes. The FTC also says the guide "will override anything in your sales contract."

The guide shows whether the car is sold "as is" or with a warranty, and what percentage of repair costs the dealer will pay if there is one. It also has a box for service contracts. A dealer that offers one checks the box beside the words "Service Contract." The FTC says dealers do not check that box if their state regulates service contracts as insurance.

## What to read before you rely on either

For a buyer, the FTC's guidance points to a short list of things to check:

1. Whether the Buyers Guide says "as is" or lists a warranty, and what the warranty covers.
2. Whether a service contract is offered, and whether the box is checked on the guide.
3. Cancellation terms, so you know what happens if you sell the car or change your mind.
4. Deductibles, which set how much you pay on each claim.
5. Whether repairs must be done at the dealer or can be done elsewhere.

The FTC adds one more rule worth knowing. If a buyer purchases a service contract from the dealer within 90 days of the purchase, "the dealer can't remove implied warranties on the systems covered in the contract." That limit is one reason the timing of a service contract sale matters.

## What this means for anyone studying auto retail

The lesson from the FTC's guidance is that disclosure is part of the product. A dealership that sells service contracts is also responsible for showing buyers, in the required form, what the contract is and what it is not. Rules like the Used Car Rule are part of the ordinary work of operating a dealership.

If you are learning how dealership businesses work, it is useful to see that the F&I office, the service department, and the sales floor are connected by the same paperwork. A buyer who understands the difference between a warranty and a service contract asks better questions. A business that explains the difference clearly has fewer misunderstandings to resolve.
