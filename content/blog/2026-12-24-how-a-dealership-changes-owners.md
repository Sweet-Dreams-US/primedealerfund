Selling a house usually involves a buyer, a seller and a lender. Selling a franchised car dealership involves a fourth party that does not own the business: the automaker. This article explains in general terms why that is, and what a public company’s annual report says about it.

It is general education about how the industry is set up. It does not describe any particular sale.

## What a franchised dealership is

Most new cars in the United States are sold by dealerships that operate under a franchise agreement with an automaker or its authorized distributor. Wikipedia’s article on car dealerships describes this arrangement, and AutoNation’s annual report says the same about its own stores: "We operate each of our new vehicle stores under a franchise agreement with a vehicle manufacturer or distributor."

The dealership is a separate business from the automaker. The owner carries the building, the staff and the inventory. The automaker supplies the vehicles and the brand, and sets rules in the franchise agreement for how the dealership operates.

## Why the automaker has a say

Because the automaker’s brand is on the building, it cares who runs it. AutoNation’s annual report says its franchise agreements impose requirements on changes in management. In the risk factors section, the company says its framework agreements "contain restrictions regarding a change in control, which may be outside of our control." It also says manufacturers can terminate a franchise for "any unapproved change of ownership or management."

That second statement explains why approval matters in practice. If a dealership changes hands without the automaker’s agreement, the franchise itself can be at risk.

## Franchise laws also shape the deal

State law is part of the picture as well. Wikipedia says that U.S. states restrict direct factory sales through franchise laws that require new cars to be sold only by licensed and bonded, independently owned dealerships. It also says all states impose severe limits on the ability of a manufacturer to terminate a franchise relationship.

AutoNation’s annual report points the same way. It says state dealership franchise laws generally require "good cause" for termination or non-renewal. So the automaker has real influence over a sale, but it works inside rules that protect dealers from arbitrary treatment. A buyer and seller who understand both sides are better prepared than one who assumes the automaker can say anything or nothing.

## Change of control is broader than a sale

A change of ownership does not have to be a full sale of the dealership. AutoNation’s annual report describes change of control triggers in its framework agreements. It lists a 20 percent stake acquired by another manufacturer, or a 50 percent stake acquired by an unaffiliated party, as events connected to rights the manufacturer or distributor may have to acquire stores at fair market value or compel a sale.

Those numbers belong to one company’s agreements and should not be read as a rule for every dealership. The general point is that the contracts look at who controls the business, not only at who signs the bill of sale. Anyone studying a dealership transaction should read what the agreement defines as a change of control.

## Other things that need approval

Ownership is not the only matter that involves the automaker. AutoNation’s annual report says dealers generally need the manufacturer to approve any relocation or the grant of a new franchise. The same report says its agreements limit how many stores of a given manufacturer the company may acquire.

Taken together, these points show that a franchise is not a free standing asset. It comes with conditions that the automaker can enforce.

## Dealerships can stay in the same hands for a long time

Wikipedia notes that new dealership licenses can be geographically restricted, and that this has led to dealerships becoming, in its words, "in essence hereditary." The existing franchise has value in part because a new one is not easy to obtain.

## What to look for when reading about a deal

If you read a press release or a filing about a dealership acquisition, a few questions help you understand it:

- Which franchises are involved, and which automakers must be considered?
- Does the document describe approval from the automaker, or leave it out?
- Is the buyer taking all of the ownership or only part of it?
- Does the agreement define a change of control, and at what percentage?
- Do state franchise laws apply to the location?

Not every document answers every question. A short press release often will not. Annual reports and legal documents are where the detail sits.

## Summary

A franchised dealership is owned by a local operator, but it operates under an agreement with an automaker. That agreement gives the automaker a say in changes of ownership or management, state franchise laws limit how that say can be used, and change of control provisions can reach beyond a simple sale. These facts come from public sources, and the specifics differ by company and state.
