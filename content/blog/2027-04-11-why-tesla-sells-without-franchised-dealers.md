Most people who buy a new car in the United States buy it from a franchised dealer. Tesla does not work that way. It sells directly to customers, and that choice has put the company at the center of a long running argument about how state law shapes car retail. This post explains how the two models differ, what a gallery is, and why the same company can sell cars one way in one state and a different way in the next.

## How most new cars are sold

A franchised dealer sells new vehicles for a manufacturer under a franchise agreement. The manufacturer builds the car and the dealer sells it, services it, and deals with the customer. The dealer is a separate business from the manufacturer, usually with its own owners, its own staff, and its own investment in the lot and the building.

An independent dealer, by contrast, does not hold a manufacturer's franchise to sell new vehicles. Independent dealers mostly sell used vehicles. The difference between the two types is the starting point for understanding the rest of this topic, because the law treats new car sales and used car sales differently.

## What a franchise law does

State auto franchise laws shape the relationship between manufacturers and dealers. A 2015 paper from the Mercatus Center describes three major restrictions: mandatory dealership licensing, terms that make it hard to terminate a dealership, and exclusive territories for existing dealers. Because state laws require new vehicles to be sold through franchised dealers, a manufacturer in many states cannot simply sell directly to the public.

The same paper says the coverage of these laws expanded significantly over the previous 30 years. In 1979, fewer than half of states regulated all three of those areas. By 2014, Maryland was the one holdout, regulating only two of the three.

## How Tesla sells

According to Wikipedia's article on the company, Tesla does not rely on franchised dealerships to sell vehicles. It sells directly through its website and a network of company-owned stores. The article describes Tesla as the first automaker in the United States to sell cars directly to consumers since the widespread adoption of the franchised dealer model.

The article reports that Tesla operates nearly 400 stores and galleries in more than 35 countries. Those locations are usually in retail shopping districts, malls, or other high-traffic areas, not next to other auto dealerships. The only detail it gives about online sales is that vehicles are sold through the company website.

## Galleries and places where direct sales are barred

Some jurisdictions, particularly in the United States, prohibit manufacturers from selling directly to consumers. In those places Tesla uses locations it calls galleries. Wikipedia quotes the company as saying that galleries educate and inform customers about its products but do not actually transact in the sale of vehicles.

The Mercatus paper makes the same general point from the legal side. It says laws in most states prevent Tesla from selling directly to consumers. It does not list which states allow direct sales, and the rules are set state by state, so anyone who needs the current answer for a particular state should check that state's current law.

:::note Worth knowing
A gallery shows the product and answers questions. In places where direct sales are prohibited, it does not complete the sale of a vehicle.
:::

## The argument over these laws

The Mercatus authors, Jerry Ellig and Jesse Martinez, argue against the laws. They say the rules protecting struggling dealerships at the expense of buyers can lead to higher prices, and that exclusive territories can let a dealer charge more without competitive pressure. They also say that bans on direct sales reduce consumer choice. The paper notes that Tesla itself believes direct sales could improve the dealership experience for electric vehicle buyers.

That is one side of a policy debate, and this post is not taking it. The useful thing to know is that the franchise system is a legal structure and not a natural law of retail. It exists because of state statutes, and different states have written them differently. A business plan that depends on how cars are sold should account for the possibility that those statutes change.

## What stays the same for any dealer

Whatever the sales model, some consumer protections do not change. The Federal Trade Commission says dealers must display a Buyers Guide on every used car they offer for sale and give it to the buyer after the sale. The guide shows whether the car is sold as is or with a warranty, the percentage of repair costs the dealer will pay under any warranty, and the dealer's contact information. The FTC adds that the Buyers Guide will override anything in the sales contract.

This applies to the dealer selling the used car, which is a reminder that a dealership is more than a place that hands over new vehicles. Used vehicles, service, and the paperwork around each sale are all part of the operation.

## What this means for someone studying dealerships

If you are learning how auto retail works, three points are worth keeping. First, the franchised dealer model is built on manufacturer agreements and state law. Second, Tesla is the best known example of a company that sells another way, and its galleries show how it adapts where direct sales are not allowed. Third, the rules are not the same everywhere and are not fixed forever.
