A franchised dealership is a business that sells new vehicles for a manufacturer under an agreement, and usually does a lot more than that. Most people see the showroom. The financial picture of the business looks different from the showroom, and it is worth understanding before you form a view on any dealership or on the industry.

This article explains where the sales dollars come from, where the profit tends to come from, and why those two answers are not the same.

## Sales and profit are not the same thing

Revenue, or sales dollars, is the total amount of money that comes in from selling things. Gross profit is what is left after the dealership subtracts what it paid for what it sold. A department can bring in a large share of the sales and a small share of the profit, or the other way around.

This matters because the headline numbers people quote about dealerships are often sales numbers. A department that sells many high priced items shows large sales. If the dealership earns only a thin margin on each item, that department will not show a matching share of profit. Reading a dealership well means keeping the two measures apart.

## What the sales mix looks like

The National Automobile Dealers Association, known as NADA, publishes yearly data on franchised light-vehicle dealers, which is the group that sells cars, SUVs and light trucks. Its figures for 2025 show the share of total dealership sales dollars by department:

- New vehicles: 54.9 percent
- Used vehicles: 31.8 percent
- Service and parts: 13.3 percent

:::note Sales share, not profit share
These NADA figures measure each department's share of total sales dollars. They do not measure profit. The 2024 figures were close: 54.7 percent new, 32.0 percent used and 13.2 percent service and parts.
:::

On sales alone, the new vehicle department looks like the center of the business. It brings in more than half of the sales dollars. That is the number most people have in mind when they picture a dealership.

## Why service and parts carry so much of the profit

The profit picture is different. The Wikipedia article on car dealerships states that dealership profits in the United States mainly come from servicing, some from used cars, and little from new cars, and it cites NADA Data 2015 for that statement. The article gives no percentages, so treat it as a general description and not a precise split.

A more recent set of figures comes from a related segment. American Truck Dealers, a division of NADA, reports gross profit by department for new-truck dealerships, which are a different business from a car dealership. For 2025, the combined service, parts and body shop department produced 77.5 percent of gross profit. The new-truck department produced 19.7 percent, and the used-truck department produced 2.0 percent. These numbers do not describe car dealerships and should not be applied to them directly. They do show the same general pattern: the department that sells the vehicle brings in much of the sales volume, and the department that maintains it supplies much of the profit.

One reason this pattern is plausible is repeat business. A vehicle is sold once, but it may need maintenance and repairs for many years. Another reason is warranty work. The Wikipedia article notes that state laws set the terms under which manufacturers pay dealers for warranty repairs.

## What used vehicles add

Used vehicles take about a third of sales dollars at franchised light-vehicle dealers, according to the NADA figures above. The sources used for this article describe used vehicles as contributing "some" profit, which is a smaller role than service and a larger role than new vehicle sales in the Wikipedia summary. For the truck segment, the used department's share of gross profit was small.

Used vehicle sales also carry consumer rules. The Federal Trade Commission explains that dealers must display a Buyers Guide in every used car they offer for sale, and the guide shows whether the car is sold "as is" or with a warranty. Rules like these shape how the department operates, but they do not by themselves tell you how much it earns.

## Finance and insurance

Many dealers also arrange financing and sell optional products such as gap insurance. The Wikipedia article lists financing and insurance among the after-sales services that dealers provide, and the Federal Trade Commission notes that dealers may add optional extras and that buyers can often skip them or buy them elsewhere.

The public sources used for this article do not give a share of dealership profit for finance and insurance, so this article does not estimate one. When you read a dealership's financial statements, look for how the business reports this line separately from vehicle sales.

## Questions worth asking about any dealership

Understanding the profit mix gives you better questions to ask when you look at a dealership as a business. These are questions, not conclusions:

- What share of gross profit comes from each department, not just what share of sales?
- How stable is the service and parts business from year to year?
- How does the dealership report finance and insurance income?
- How much does it depend on a single manufacturer's franchise?

Sales dollars tell you where customers spend money. Gross profit tells you where the dealership keeps it. The two lists can look quite different, and the department that looks small on the sales chart can matter a great deal to the bottom line.
