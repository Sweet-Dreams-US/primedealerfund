A trade-in looks like a small step inside a car purchase. A customer hands over keys to an old vehicle and takes a credit toward a new one. Inside a dealership, that step does more work than it seems to. It ties the new vehicle line to the used vehicle line, and one customer deal can end up feeding both.

This article explains how that link works. It is general education about how dealerships operate, built from public sources. It is not a prediction about any dealership or investment.

## What a trade-in is

A trade-in happens when a customer sells their current vehicle to the dealership as part of buying another one. The dealership values the old vehicle, and that value is applied to the price of the vehicle the customer is buying. The customer sees one transaction. The dealership sees two: a sale of a vehicle, and a purchase of a vehicle.

That second part is what matters for the business. The dealership now owns a vehicle it did not have to find at an auction or buy from a stranger. Each trade-in is a vehicle the dealership took in directly from a customer.

## One customer, two lines

Dealerships track their business in lines. New vehicle sales are one line. Used vehicle sales are another. A customer who buys a new vehicle and trades in an older one touches both on the same day.

The new vehicle sale is recorded in the new line. The traded vehicle becomes used inventory. If the dealership later sells it to a different customer, that sale is recorded in the used line. So the first customer's visit produced a new vehicle sale today and used vehicle inventory that may sell later.

This is why new and used sales are not two separate businesses sitting side by side. A strong new vehicle showroom can supply the used lot. A used lot with good inventory can give customers a reason to come in and trade up.

## Where used inventory comes from

A dealership can get used vehicles in several ways. Trade-ins are one. Auctions are another. Public companies describe their own mix in their annual reports.

:::note What a large dealership group says
AutoNation's 2025 annual report says it generally acquires used vehicles from customers, "primarily through trade-ins and our 'We'll Buy Your Car' program," as well as through auctions, lease terminations and other sources. It also says it can self-source a significant portion of its used inventory through that program.
:::

The phrase to notice is "from customers." It shows that a large dealership group treats customers as a supply of used vehicles, and that the supply is not limited to people buying a new vehicle that day. The same report mentions a program for buying vehicles from customers outright.

## What happens after the trade arrives

A traded vehicle is not ready to sell the moment it arrives. AutoNation's report says it generally reconditions used vehicles acquired for retail sale in its parts and service departments.

This is another link between lines. The service and parts departments do work on the vehicle before the used line can sell it. So a single trade-in can involve the new vehicle line, the used vehicle line, and the service and parts departments.

Not every traded vehicle goes to the retail lot. The same report says used vehicles that are not sold at the dealership's stores are generally sold at wholesale prices through auctions. A dealership has to decide which vehicles to keep for retail and which to pass along. That decision depends on the vehicle's condition and what the dealership expects it to sell for.

## Why the used line matters to the whole business

The used line is large. The National Automobile Dealers Association reports in its 2025 data that used vehicles were 31.8 percent of total dealership sales dollars in 2025, compared with 32.0 percent in 2024.

:::note A share of the whole
NADA's 2025 full-year report puts used vehicles at 31.8 percent of total dealership sales dollars, down slightly from 32.0 percent in 2024. This is a share of sales dollars. The report does not give a share of profit.
:::

That share is worth keeping in mind. If nearly a third of sales dollars come from used vehicles, then the way a dealership sources those vehicles matters to the business. Trade-ins are one of the main ways the new line supports the used line.

## How to read this when you study a dealership

If you are learning how dealerships work, a few plain questions follow from the link between new and used sales:

- How does the dealership get its used vehicles: trade-ins, purchases from customers, auctions, or a mix?
- When a customer trades in, does the vehicle go to the retail lot or to wholesale?
- What work is done on the vehicle in the service department before it is sold?
- How do the new and used lines show up in the dealership's reported results?

These questions do not have one answer for every store. A large public group and a single store may source vehicles differently. Reading a dealership's own reports, and reading NADA's industry data, gives you a way to compare.
