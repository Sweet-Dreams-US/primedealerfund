A dealership earns money in more than one place. Most people think of the sticker price on a vehicle, but the paperwork signed after the price is agreed can also produce income for the dealer. That paperwork is called finance and insurance, usually shortened to F&I. This article explains what F&I covers and where it shows up when a dealership group reports its results.

## What F&I actually includes

In its annual report to the SEC, AutoNation describes F&I as two things. The first is arranging vehicle financing through third-party finance sources. The second is selling vehicle service and other protection products.

The same filing lists the protection products: extended service contracts, maintenance programs, GAP, tire and wheel protection, and theft protection. GAP covers the gap between what a customer still owes on a loan and what the insurance company pays after a casualty such as a total loss.

:::note Worth knowing
AutoNation's 10-K says it is paid by third-party lenders for arranging financing, and that its exposure to loss on those arrangements is generally limited to the commissions it receives.
:::

## How the income is earned

F&I income is mostly commission. A lender pays the dealer for arranging a loan. A protection product is sold on a commission basis. For certain products, AutoNation says it also takes part in future underwriting profit under what it calls retrospective commission arrangements. That means some income can depend on how a pool of contracts performs after the sale.

This is why F&I is different from selling a vehicle. F&I income arrives as a commission on something a third party provides, so it takes a slightly different eye to read than vehicle sales.

## Where it appears in a dealer report

In AutoNation's 10-K, F&I is not its own reportable segment. The company reports three segments: Domestic, Import, and Premium Luxury. Each one sells F&I products alongside vehicles. So a reader looking for an F&I section in the segment notes will not find one.

The place to look is the revenue and gross profit tables in the earnings release. In AutoNation's full year 2025 results, the line is labeled "Finance and insurance, net." The company reported $1,464.4 million for the year, up from $1,360.1 million in 2024. In that table, F&I revenue and F&I gross profit show the same number.

## A per-vehicle figure

Beyond the dollar total, a report usually gives a ratio that makes departments easier to compare. For F&I, that ratio is the per-vehicle figure.

Dealers also report F&I as a gross profit per vehicle retailed, often shortened to PVR. AutoNation's 2025 figure was $2,769, compared with $2,612 in 2024, an increase of $157.

A per-vehicle number is useful because it lets you compare a small store and a large group on the same footing. It is also easy to misread. The number counts every vehicle retailed, including those where the customer paid cash or bought nothing extra. It is an average across all of them, not what one customer paid.

:::note Worth knowing
AutoNation's 10-K states that it expects income from arranging financing to shift to AutoNation Finance, its captive finance company, which would reduce F&I gross profit. The company expects that to be offset by AutoNation Finance profitability.
:::

## Why the line can move without a change in sales

The AutoNation example shows one way a report can change shape. When a dealer group starts its own finance company, interest income on the loans is recognized over the contractual term of the loans. The earnings release shows AutoNation Finance income of $9.8 million for 2025, reported separately from F&I.

For a reader, the lesson is practical. If F&I gross profit moves, ask whether sales volume moved, whether the product mix moved, or whether the way the company records the income moved. The report's notes are where those answers sit.

## How to read it next time

A short checklist helps when you open a dealer report for the first time.

First, find the F&I line in the gross profit table. Second, check whether the report gives a per-vehicle figure and compare it to the prior year. Third, read the notes on how commissions and any underwriting participation are recognized. Fourth, check whether a captive finance company is reported separately. Fifth, keep F&I in context with the other departments. The NADA 2025 full-year report, for example, shows the share of total dealership sales dollars as 54.9% new vehicles, 31.8% used vehicles, and 13.3% service and parts. Those are shares of sales dollars, which is a different measure from gross profit, so do not compare them directly with an F&I gross profit figure.
