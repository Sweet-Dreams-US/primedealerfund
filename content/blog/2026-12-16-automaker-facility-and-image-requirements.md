Walk into two dealerships of the same brand in different cities and they often look alike. The same signs, similar showroom layouts, matching materials. That is not a coincidence. Automakers write standards for how their dealerships look and what they contain, and a dealer agrees to meet those standards when it signs a franchise agreement. This article explains what those requirements are, how they work and why the cost matters to dealers and to anyone studying the business.

## Where the requirements come from

A franchised dealer sells under the automaker's name. The automaker wants customers to have a consistent experience of the brand wherever they shop. To protect that, it sets conditions for the facility.

AutoNation, a large public dealership group, describes this in its 2025 annual report filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission. It says its franchise agreements impose numerous requirements and restrictions, including on showroom and service facilities and signage. It also says automakers can influence the naming and marketing of its stores and the level at which it capitalizes them.

:::note From a public filing
AutoNation's 2025 annual report states that manufacturers impose minimum facility requirements that can require significant capital expenditures.
:::

## What the standards usually cover

The requirements fall into a few practical areas. Signage and exterior design come first, because they are the most visible part of the brand. Showroom layout, finishes and fixtures come next. Service areas are also covered, including the number of service bays and the equipment in them. Parking and customer areas can be part of the standard too.

The same annual report notes that dealers may use manufacturer trademarks, logos and designs at their stores and in advertising, subject to certain restrictions. Naming is also limited. AutoNation says its framework agreements restrict its ability to name and brand its stores.

## Three kinds of facility program

A research report sponsored by the National Automobile Dealers Association and the Canadian Automobile Dealers Association studied automaker facility programs in 2013. Its author, Glenn Mercer, sorted them into three layers.

Expansion adds space, such as service bays, parking or showroom room. Modernization updates exteriors, fixtures and equipment. Standardization makes stores of the same brand match in materials and layout.

The report's author drew a distinction between them. In a view he quotes from an industry advisor, expansion can add value, modernization can maintain it, and standardization can reduce it. That is one observer's opinion, but it shows why dealers do not all react to these programs the same way.

## What the programs cost

The report gives a sense of scale. It describes a program costing under $100,000 as hard to imagine, and outlays above $5,000,000 as common. It estimated dealer facility investment at $10 billion to $15 billion a year across the United States and Canada. It also reported that dealers saw the cost of automaker directed modernization running 20 to 30 percent above what they would have spent on their own.

:::note A dated figure
These numbers come from a report published in February 2013. Costs and programs have changed since, so treat them as an illustration of scale and not as current prices.
:::

The report also describes costs that are easy to miss. A larger building can bring higher property taxes, insurance and maintenance, and sometimes added staff. Its example of a $1 million showroom overhaul needing about 60 extra new vehicle sales a year, at a $30,000 price, just to recover its cost shows how much sales volume a project can require.

How many dealers take part also varies. The report put participation in the middle of 2012 at roughly 75 percent of Asian and European brand stores and roughly 50 percent of domestic brand stores. It quotes one dealer who called standardization costs "just the franchise tax I have to pay." That remark shows how some dealers see these programs: as a price of the franchise and not as an investment they chose.

## When upgrades pay off and when they do not

Results vary. The report found the strongest returns when a run down store was renovated, with returns of around 30 percent or more in some cases. Service expansion generally paid off if the new bays could be filled. Modernization often showed little payback unless the automaker covered much of the cost.

One automaker's anonymous data in the report showed upgraded dealers returning to their earlier earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization in about two years and to earlier net income in about four. Dealer approval of the programs was mixed, with participating dealers rating them higher than those who had not taken part.

## Why this matters

Facility requirements are a cost of holding a franchise. They keep the brand consistent for the automaker, and they put capital demands on the dealer. For anyone looking at how dealerships are run, the questions to ask are simple. What does the franchise agreement require? When is the next upgrade due? Does the automaker share the cost? How long would the investment take to earn back?
