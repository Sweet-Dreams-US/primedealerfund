When people picture a car dealership, they usually picture the cars on the lot. That is only part of what the business holds. A franchised dealership is a mix of vehicles, parts, equipment, buildings, land and a contract with a manufacturer, and each of those behaves differently when you try to understand what the business is worth.

This article breaks a dealership into its main pieces. It is general education about how these businesses are put together. It is not advice about any investment.

## The scale of the industry

The National Automobile Dealers Association, or NADA, publishes industry data. Its mid-year 2026 report counts 16,991 franchised light-vehicle dealers in the United States. NADA reports that in the first half of 2026 those dealers sold 7.9 million light-duty vehicles and that total franchised dealership sales topped $640 billion.

:::note Sales are not only vehicles
NADA's mid-year 2026 report says dealerships wrote more than 136 million repair orders in the first half of the year, with service and parts sales of nearly $83 billion. A dealership earns money from more than selling cars, and that shapes what it needs to own.
:::

## Vehicle inventory

Inventory is the most visible asset. New and used vehicles sit on the lot waiting to be sold. They are worth a lot of money in total, and they are also the asset that changes the fastest, because vehicles are bought and sold every day.

A guide on buying and selling dealerships, written by Erika Gagne, CPA, for the magazine of the New Hampshire Automobile Dealers Association, makes a useful distinction. It says new vehicles usually transfer at invoice, while used vehicle valuation depends on market conditions and aging. It also says a buyer should closely assess inventory quality to avoid assuming aged or overpriced stock.

In plain terms, a new vehicle has a clear starting price from the manufacturer. A used vehicle is worth what the market says it is worth today, and a car that has sat for a long time is usually worth less than one that just arrived.

## Floor plan financing

Most of that inventory is not paid for with the dealer's own cash. Dealers commonly use a floor plan line, which is a loan used to finance the vehicles on the lot. That matters because it means the inventory on the balance sheet is partly offset by money owed to a lender.

The article from Kerrigan Advisors describes what happens to this loan when a dealership is sold. The seller's floor plan line is paid off at the sale. The buyer needs enough floor plan capacity to take on the seller's vehicles. If the two dealers use different lenders, the buyer's lender expands its financing and the seller's lender is repaid to close out the balance.

So when someone talks about the value of a dealership's inventory, there is a second question to ask. How much of it is financed?

## Parts and accessories

A dealership also holds parts and accessories for its service and repair work. The Gagne guide says accurate reconciliation of parts, accessories and prepaid items is essential when a dealership changes hands. Parts are a smaller asset than vehicles, but they are tied directly to the service business that NADA's numbers show to be so large.

## Equipment and tangible assets

A dealership also uses equipment to do its work, such as tools and machinery in the service department. The Gagne guide groups assets like these under the broader heading of tangible assets, and it mentions facility condition as something a buyer should look at. Equipment wears out, so its condition matters.

## Land and buildings

The showroom, the service bays and the lot sit on real estate. Who owns that real estate varies. The Gagne guide says real estate is often held separately from the operating entity. When that is the case, the buyer and seller negotiate lease terms, purchase options and facility requirements as their own matter, apart from the business itself.

Kerrigan Advisors describes acquisitions as typically including all assets, with real estate, vehicle inventory and the value of the business itself named together. The buyer funds the purchase of the building and land along with the value of the franchise.

The takeaway is that the dealership business and the property it operates from may be two different things, owned by the same group or by different ones. Anyone reading about a dealership should find out which arrangement applies.

## Goodwill and the franchise

Not everything a dealership owns can be touched. The Gagne guide states that dealership value rests on tangible assets and intangible goodwill, which the industry calls blue sky. Blue sky is the part of a dealership's value that goes beyond its tangible assets.

The franchise is closely related. A dealer sells a particular manufacturer's vehicles under an agreement with that manufacturer. The Gagne guide discusses the franchise through the manufacturer's approval of a sale and notes that buyers must determine whether the franchise aligns with their strategic objectives. Kerrigan Advisors likewise refers to the intrinsic value of the franchise itself as something a buyer pays for.

:::note Value that does not sit on the lot
Both the goodwill and the franchise are part of what a buyer pays for, but neither is a thing you can park or store. This is why two dealerships with similar inventory and buildings can be valued very differently.
:::

## Putting it together

A franchised dealership holds vehicles that turn over quickly and are often financed through a floor plan line. It holds parts and equipment for its service department. It operates from land and buildings that may be owned by the business or held separately. And it carries intangible value in its goodwill and its franchise. When someone tells you what a dealership is worth, it helps to ask which of these pieces they are counting, and which they are leaving out.

Understanding the pieces does not tell you whether any particular dealership or investment is a good one. That depends on facts about the specific business, its debts, its property arrangements and its contracts.
