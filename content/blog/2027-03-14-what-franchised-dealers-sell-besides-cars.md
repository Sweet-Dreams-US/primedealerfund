When most people picture a car dealership, they picture the showroom floor and the cars on it. A franchised dealership is a larger business than that. It sells new vehicles, sells used vehicles, repairs vehicles, sells parts, and helps customers pay for what they buy.

This article walks through those other lines of business. Anyone who wants to understand how a dealership works, including people who study the industry as investors, needs to see the whole picture and not only the cars.

This article is general education about the auto retail industry. It is not investment advice and it is not an offer to sell securities.

## The dealership is a group of departments

A franchised dealer holds an agreement with a manufacturer to sell that brand's new vehicles. In practice the store runs as several departments under one roof: new vehicle sales, used vehicle sales, service and parts, and a finance office.

Each department has its own customers, its own staff and its own rhythm. A buyer may walk in once every few years to buy a car. A service customer may come back several times a year for repairs. Seeing the departments separately is the first step to understanding how the business earns money.

## Used vehicles are a second sales line

The National Automobile Dealers Association publishes yearly figures for franchised dealerships. For 2025, new vehicles made up 54.9 percent of total dealership sales dollars, and used vehicles made up 31.8 percent.

:::note Worth knowing
NADA reports total franchised light-vehicle dealership sales of about $1.3 trillion in 2025. New vehicles were 54.9 percent of those sales dollars, used vehicles 31.8 percent, and service and parts 13.3 percent. These are shares of sales dollars, not shares of profit.
:::

That last point matters. Sales dollars show how much money moves through a department. They do not show how much of it the dealership keeps. A department that is a small share of sales can still be an important part of the business, and the NADA figures above do not say how profit is divided.

## Service and parts

Service and parts is the department that repairs and maintains vehicles and sells the parts used in the work. NADA reports it at 13.3 percent of total dealership sales dollars in 2025, up from 13.2 percent in 2024. NADA also reports service and parts sales of $164.6 billion across all dealerships, or about $9.7 million for the average dealership.

Two things set this department apart from vehicle sales. First, it serves customers who already own a car, so it does not depend only on new buyers walking in. Second, it can bring the same customer back many times over the life of a vehicle. This is general reasoning and not a figure from the sources, but it is the reason many people who study dealerships look closely at this department.

## Financing

Most buyers do not pay for a car all at once. The Federal Trade Commission explains that buyers can pay in full or finance over time, and that financing raises the total cost because the buyer also pays for credit, including interest.

Dealers, banks, credit unions and finance companies all offer financing, and the terms differ. The FTC tells buyers to compare offers. It also warns that low monthly payments often come with longer loan periods and higher interest rates, which can make the total cost much higher.

For the dealership, the finance office is where this work happens. For the customer, the FTC advises confirming the exact car price, the total sales price with financing, the finance charge, the APR, and the number and amount of payments before signing.

NADA's report does not give the finance and insurance share of sales or profit, so this article does not put a number on it.

## Add-on products and service contracts

The finance office is also where a dealer may offer optional products. The FTC lists gap insurance, VIN etching and rustproofing as examples of add-ons. It says add-ons can cost thousands of dollars and are often raised at the end of the sales process.

The FTC adds that a buyer can usually buy the vehicle without add-ons, and that most can be bought from independent retailers. A buyer who wants one is advised to ask what each costs and what it includes, and to get the answers in writing.

:::note Worth knowing
The FTC says a service contract is often called an extended warranty, but it is not a warranty. It is a promise to perform or pay for certain repairs or services. Manufacturers, dealers and independent companies all sell service contracts, and prices and coverage vary widely.
:::

The same FTC page explains the Buyers Guide. A dealer must display one on every used car offered for sale. It shows whether the car is sold as is or with a warranty, and what percentage of repair costs the dealer will pay under the warranty.

## Why this matters to someone studying dealerships

Put these pieces together and a dealership looks like a business with several sources of revenue: new vehicle sales, used vehicle sales, service and parts, financing, and optional products. Each has its own customers, its own rules and its own risks.

That is a reason to look beyond the car count when you study a dealership or a group of them. Ask which departments the store has, how large each one is, and how the store is run day to day. Sales share is not the same as profit share, and a figure from one dealership does not describe another.
