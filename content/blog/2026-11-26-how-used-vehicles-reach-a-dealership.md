Used vehicles are the second largest part of a franchised dealership's sales. They also work differently from new vehicles, because a dealer cannot simply order them from a manufacturer. The dealer has to find each used vehicle, decide what it is worth, prepare it and then sell it. This article explains how used vehicles reach a dealership and what happens to them next. It is written for readers who are learning how dealerships work, including people who are thinking about investing in the sector.

## Trade-ins are the main source

AutoNation, a large public dealer group, describes its used vehicle sourcing in its annual report to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. It says it generally acquires used vehicles from customers, primarily through trade-ins.

A trade-in happens when a customer hands over their current vehicle as part of buying another one. The dealer values the old vehicle and takes ownership of it. This ties used inventory to new vehicle sales, since a customer who buys one vehicle often leaves another behind.

:::note Worth knowing
AutoNation's annual report says it generally acquires used vehicles from customers, primarily through trade-ins, as well as through auctions, lease terminations and other sources.
:::

## Auctions, lease returns and other sources

Trade-ins are not the only route. The same report names auctions, lease terminations and other sources as additional ways to acquire used vehicles.

An auction is a sale where dealers and other approved buyers bid on vehicles. A lease termination happens when a leased vehicle comes to the end of its lease and is returned. The report does not say how many vehicles come from each source, so a reader should not assume any particular split. What it shows is that a dealer has several ways to restock its used inventory, and that trade-ins come first.

## Reconditioning before the sale

A vehicle taken in on trade is rarely ready to sell the same day. AutoNation says it generally reconditions the used vehicles it acquires for retail sale in its parts and service departments.

Reconditioning means getting a vehicle ready to be sold to a retail buyer. This is one place where the departments of a dealership work together. The service department does more than repair customer vehicles. It also prepares the dealer's own inventory for sale.

## Retail versus wholesale

Not every used vehicle ends up on the dealer's own lot for sale to the public. AutoNation says that used vehicles it does not sell at its stores are generally sold at wholesale prices through auctions.

Retail means selling to an individual customer at the dealership. Wholesale means selling the vehicle at a wholesale price, in this case through an auction, instead of to a customer. The report states the general practice. It does not say which vehicles go to wholesale or why, so a reader should not guess at the reasons for any single sale.

:::note Worth knowing
In NADA's 2026 midyear report, used vehicles sold by new-vehicle dealerships had an average retail price of $29,281.
:::

## How big used vehicles are inside a dealership

NADA, the National Automobile Dealers Association, publishes yearly industry figures. In its 2026 midyear report, used vehicles were 32.8 percent of total franchised dealership sales dollars, up from 32.6 percent in 2025. New vehicles were 53.6 percent, and service, parts and body shop were 13.6 percent.

These are shares of sales dollars. They do not show how much profit each part of the business keeps, so they should not be read as profit shares. They do show that used vehicles are a large part of what a franchised dealership sells, and that the share moved only slightly between the two periods.

## Why the used side matters to an investor

A dealer group that sources used vehicles well has more to sell to retail customers and more work for its service department. A dealer group that sources them poorly may end up wholesaling vehicles it hoped to sell at retail. These are possibilities to look into, not conclusions. The public filings of a dealer group are where its own description of used vehicle sourcing, reconditioning and wholesale can be read.

## What this means when you study a dealership

Used vehicles connect several parts of a dealership. A trade-in links used inventory to new vehicle sales. Reconditioning links it to the service and parts departments. Wholesale links it to the auction market. A few questions follow for anyone learning how a dealer group works. Where does the store get its used vehicles? How much preparation does each vehicle need? What does it do with the vehicles it does not sell at retail?

The answers differ from store to store, and a dealer group's own reports are the place to look for them. Reading how a public dealer group such as AutoNation describes its own sourcing is a useful way to see the real process in the dealer's own words.
