A public auto dealer group files an annual report with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. The form is called a 10-K. It is long, and most readers do not need every page of it. A few sections explain how a franchised dealer group is built and where its revenue comes from. This post walks through those sections using the AutoNation 10-K for fiscal year 2025 as the example.

## Start with the Business section

Item 1 of a 10-K is titled Business. The company describes itself here in its own words. It covers the company's segments, strategy, operations, regulation, competition, and employees.

For AutoNation, this section says the company is one of the largest U.S. automotive retailers. It reports 323 new vehicle franchises from 245 stores, mainly in Sunbelt metro markets. The same section lists 52 collision centers, 26 AutoNation USA used vehicle stores, 4 auction operations, 3 parts distribution centers, and an auto finance company.

That list matters because a dealer group is more than showrooms. Collision centers, used vehicle stores, auctions, parts distribution, and finance are all separate parts of the business. When you read any dealer group report, make a short list of every type of operation the company says it runs. It gives you a map before you look at any numbers.

:::note Worth knowing
The filing counts 323 new vehicle franchises across 245 stores, so some stores carry more than one franchise. A franchise is the agreement to sell a manufacturer's brand, so the franchise count and the store count are two different measures.
:::

## Learn the segments

A 10-K groups results into reportable segments. AutoNation reports four: Domestic, Import, Premium Luxury, and AutoNation Finance. The first three are groups of manufacturer brands. The fourth is the company's finance arm.

The filing also gives each franchise group's share of total revenue. Premium Luxury is about 37 percent, Import is about 30 percent, and Domestic is about 27 percent. Reading these shares shows you where the company's revenue is concentrated. A group with a large share of revenue from one type of brand depends more on the health of those brands than a group spread evenly.

When you compare two dealer groups, compare how they split their segments. Two companies with the same total revenue can have very different mixes.

## Read the risk factors for what the company names

Item 1A is titled Risk Factors. Here the company lists the material risks to its business, its financial condition, and its stock. The company chooses what to list, so the section tells you what management considers important enough to disclose.

A useful habit is to read this section for the company's own words and then compare it with the prior year's report. A risk that is new, or one that has moved to the top of the list, is worth a closer look. A risk that has quietly disappeared is also worth asking about.

## Use the MD&A to see why numbers moved

Item 7 is Management's Discussion and Analysis, usually called the MD&A. It gives management's view of results, liquidity, and capital resources. In the AutoNation report it begins on page 26.

The MD&A is where numbers get explained. Instead of only seeing that new vehicle revenue changed, you read management's account of why. Look for the lines that make up a dealership's revenue: new vehicles, used vehicles, parts and service, and finance and insurance.

The AutoNation filing reports new vehicle revenue of about $13,501.3 million on 259,264 retail units. Dividing one by the other gives roughly $52,000 of new vehicle revenue for each retail unit. That is a rough figure from simple division, but it shows how a unit count and a revenue total can be turned into a number you can compare across years or across companies.

Units and revenue per unit are two ways to read the same business. A company can sell fewer units and still grow revenue if each unit brings in more, and the MD&A is where management tells you which one happened.

## Finish with the financial statements and notes

Item 8 holds the audited financial statements and the notes that go with them. In the AutoNation report it begins on page 56. The statements show the numbers. The notes explain how the company arrived at them and what sits behind them.

Many readers skip the notes. If you only have time for one part of Item 8, the notes are often worth more than another look at the totals, because they describe the company's accounting choices and obligations in detail.

## Read two years side by side

One report tells you where a company is. Two reports tell you where it is going. Open the current and prior year's 10-K together and compare the same sections:

First, compare the Business section for changes in store counts, franchises, and types of operations. Second, compare the segment mix to see whether revenue shifted between brand groups. Third, compare the risk factors for anything added or removed. Fourth, read the MD&A for the reasons behind the main changes.

This takes less time than reading one report from front to back, and it surfaces the questions worth asking.

## A short checklist

Before you close a dealer group report, you should be able to answer a few things in plain words. What does this company own and run? Which brand groups drive its revenue? What risks does it name? Why did its main numbers move? If you can answer those four questions, you have read the report well enough to compare it with the next one.
