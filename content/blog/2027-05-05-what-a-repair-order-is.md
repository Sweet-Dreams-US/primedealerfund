Every time a car comes in for service at a dealership, someone opens a repair order. It is a short document, often one or two pages, and it is the basic unit of record for the service department. If you want to understand how a dealership service department earns money, the repair order is the place to start.

This article explains what a repair order is, what is written on it, how it moves through a service visit, and why it matters to anyone studying dealerships as a business.

## What a repair order is

A repair order, often shortened to RO, is the written record of a service visit. It is opened when the customer arrives and closed when the work is finished and paid for. Each visit gets its own number, so the dealership can find the job later, match it to a vehicle, and match it to a charge.

The same document does several jobs. It is the customer's authorization for the work. It is the technician's instruction for what to do. It is the accounting record for the labor and parts that were sold. And it is the history of what has been done to that vehicle.

## What is written on it

The top of a repair order holds the basics: the customer's name, address and phone number, the make and model of the vehicle, the mileage when it came in, and often the license plate. A good order also records how the customer prefers to be contacted, so the service advisor can reach them if the job changes.

The body of the order describes the work. Industry writers who teach service advisors often use a set of four words for this part: concern, cause, correction, and confirmation.

- The concern is the problem as the customer describes it, written in the customer's own words.
- The cause is what the technician found after testing.
- The correction is the work that was done.
- The confirmation is a note that the problem was resolved.

The order also lists the parts used and the labor charged, and it closes with the name of the service advisor who handled the visit.

:::note Worth knowing
Auto Service World reports that a clear repair order helps customers understand their charges and whether the work was done, and that writing the concern in the customer's own words shows the customer they were heard.
:::

## How an order moves through a visit

A visit usually follows the same path. The customer arrives and describes the concern. The service advisor opens the order and writes it down. The vehicle goes to a technician, who inspects it and reports what is needed. The advisor explains the work and the cost to the customer and gets approval. The technician does the work, the order is updated with the parts and labor, and the customer pays when the vehicle is returned.

Each step leaves a mark on the order. That is why a complete order can answer almost any question about a visit afterward: what the customer asked for, what was found, what was approved, who did the work, and what it cost.

## Labor and parts: the two lines that matter

Most of the money on a repair order falls into two groups. Labor is the time the technician spends on the job, charged at the shop's hourly rate. Parts are the items installed on the vehicle, charged at the price the dealership sets.

Some orders also include other charges, such as shop supplies or sublet work sent to an outside specialist. Dealerships track these lines separately because each one has a different cost behind it. A labor hour has a technician's pay behind it. A part has a purchase cost behind it. Keeping them apart lets the dealership see what each visit actually contributed.

## Why the number of orders matters

Because every visit produces an order, the count of repair orders is a simple measure of how busy a service department is. NADA, the National Automobile Dealers Association, publishes this figure for the industry. Its 2025 full-year report says dealerships wrote more than 276 million repair orders, with service and parts sales exceeding $164 billion.

Those two numbers can be read together. More orders means more vehicles coming through the service lane. Dividing sales by orders gives a rough sense of how much a typical visit brings in. When someone says a service department is growing, they usually mean one of two things: more orders, or more money on each order.

## What the order tells a student of dealerships

Dealerships earn money in more than one department. The Prime Dealer Fund site describes service and parts as profit centers alongside new and used vehicle sales and finance and insurance. Service is different from sales in one important way. A vehicle is sold once, but it can come back for service many times over its life, and each return visit creates a new repair order.

That is why people who study dealerships read repair orders and the reports built from them. A single order tells you about one visit. A thousand of them tell you about the customers a dealership keeps, the kinds of work it does, and how its service lane is run.

Questions worth asking when you look at order data include how many orders the store writes each month, how much labor and parts each order averages, and whether customers come back. These are the same questions a dealership manager asks, and they are a sound starting point for anyone learning how the business works.

## Putting it together

A repair order is a plain document with a clear job. It records what the customer asked for, what was found, what was done, and what it cost. Because there is one for every service visit, the orders add up to a detailed picture of a service department's work.

If you are new to dealership operations, learning to read a repair order is a good first step. The next lessons build on it by looking at how service revenue is measured across a whole department.
