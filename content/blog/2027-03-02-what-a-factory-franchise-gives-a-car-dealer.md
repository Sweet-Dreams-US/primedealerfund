Drive past a row of car lots and they can look alike. Underneath, they run on different arrangements. Some are franchised dealerships tied to a single automaker. Others are independent lots that sell whatever they buy. This article explains what a factory franchise is, what it gives a dealer, and why state law is a large part of the story.

## What a franchise agreement is

A franchised dealership sells new or used cars under a franchise agreement with an automaker or the automaker's authorized distributor. That is how Wikipedia's article on car dealerships describes the model. The dealer is a separate business from the manufacturer, but it sells and services that manufacturer's vehicles under a contract.

The details of each agreement differ by brand and by dealer, and the public reference sources do not list every term. What they do cover is the legal frame around these agreements, which is where most of the difference between franchised and independent dealers comes from.

## What a franchised dealer sells besides cars

Selling a car is only part of the business. According to the same article, franchised dealerships often provide after-sales services such as maintenance, repairs, financing, insurance and spare parts. A customer may come in to buy a vehicle and return for years for service.

The article also says that in the United States most dealership profit comes from servicing, some from used cars, and little from new cars. That is a general description from a reference source, not a figure for any one dealership. It does explain why a dealership's service department matters to how the whole business is judged.

:::

## Why new cars are sold through dealers

Most US states restrict direct sales from a manufacturer to a consumer. Wikipedia reports that, as a result, new cars generally must be sold by licensed, bonded, independently owned dealers, and that by 2010 all 50 states and the District of Columbia had such laws.

This is why a person cannot usually buy a new car from the factory the way they can buy many other products. The sale has to pass through a licensed dealer. The same article notes that Tesla, which chose not to use franchised dealers, operates galleries in city centers and takes orders only online in the US, where those laws limit direct sales.

## What the law does for dealers

State franchise laws also protect the dealers who hold a franchise. Wikipedia reports two main protections. Most states restrict new dealerships that would compete with existing ones. Manufacturers also face strict limits on ending a franchise, and most states require them to buy back inventory when a dealership closes.

For a dealer, those protections add stability. A dealer that invests in a building, staff and inventory has some assurance that the manufacturer cannot simply cancel the arrangement or place a competing store next door without limits.

The article is also clear that this view is not shared by everyone. It says economists describe these protections as rent-seeking that raises prices for consumers. A fair reading is that the laws help the dealers who hold franchises, and that critics argue buyers pay for that.

:::note Both sides of the franchise laws
Wikipedia reports that state laws limit termination of franchises and competing dealerships, and that some economists describe the protections as rent-seeking that raises consumer prices.
:::

## How this compares with an independent dealer

An independent used car dealer is not tied to one automaker. Wikipedia mentions independent and multibrand dealers mainly in the context of franchise laws, which it says blocked direct factory sales and helped such dealers survive. The reference sources used here do not describe how independent dealers buy their cars, so this article does not either.

One rule applies to both kinds of dealer when they sell used vehicles. The Federal Trade Commission says dealers must post a Buyers Guide on every used vehicle they offer and give the buyer a copy after the sale. The Guide states whether the car is sold as is or with a warranty, and the FTC notes it overrides the sales contract. A franchised dealer selling a used car follows the same rule as an independent lot.

:::note The Buyers Guide
The Federal Trade Commission says dealers must post a Buyers Guide on every used vehicle, and that it states whether the car is sold as is or with a warranty.
:::

## What to take from this

The franchised model has three features worth remembering. The dealer sells under a contract with an automaker. The dealer earns from more than car sales, with service playing a large part according to the reference source. And state law shapes how new cars reach buyers and how franchises can be ended or added.

Independent dealers work without a manufacturer contract, but they still operate under consumer rules such as the Buyers Guide. If you are comparing two dealerships, ask which model each one follows. The answer affects what they can sell, what service they offer and what protections apply.
