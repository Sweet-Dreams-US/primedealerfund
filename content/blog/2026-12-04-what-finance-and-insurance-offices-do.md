Many dealerships have a finance and insurance office, usually shortened to F&I. For a person learning how a franchised dealership earns money, F&I is worth understanding, because it is one of the dealership's separate lines of business.

This article explains what an F&I office does and how it is paid. It is general education about the auto retail industry. It is not investment advice, and it is not an offer to sell any security.

## Where F&I sits in the dealership

A dealership is not one business. It is several departments under one roof. New vehicle sales, used vehicle sales, parts and service, and finance and insurance are the four lines that a large public retailer such as AutoNation presents in its annual report. Its 10-K for fiscal year 2025 includes charts showing each line's contribution to total revenue and gross profit.

AutoNation uses the term "finance and insurance" for these products and also calls them "Customer Financial Services." The F&I office handles the financing and the protection products that go with a vehicle sale.

## What the office arranges

The first job is financing. For buyers who finance a purchase, the F&I office arranges the loan or lease. AutoNation says it primarily arranges installment loans or leases with third-party lenders. In that arrangement, the third party is the lender, and the dealer arranges the deal.

The second job is selling protection products. AutoNation lists these examples: extended service contracts, maintenance programs, GAP, tire and wheel protection, and theft protection. It says some are AutoNation-branded, while third parties underwrite and administer them.

The exact terms differ by product and by provider. A buyer should read the contract for what it covers and what it costs. That is a point about the product, not a recommendation to buy or skip one.

## How the dealership is paid

The way F&I earns revenue is where it differs from selling a car.

On financing, AutoNation says it receives a commission payable to it from the lender. It also says its exposure to loss generally is limited to the commissions that it receives. In other words, the dealer is paid for arranging the loan, and the lender carries the credit risk of the loan.

On protection products, AutoNation says it sells the products on a commission basis. It adds that it also participates in future underwriting profit on certain products through what it calls retrospective commission arrangements with the issuers of those products.

:::note Worth knowing
AutoNation also runs a captive finance arm, AutoNation Finance, which originates and services consumer auto loans. The company reports it as a separate segment. It says that as financing shifts to that arm, it expects the resulting decrease in finance and insurance gross profit to be offset by AutoNation Finance profitability. F&I itself is not a separate reportable segment in the filing.
:::

This is a good reminder that one company's structure is not every dealer's structure. A larger group may operate its own finance company, as AutoNation does.

## Why investors look at F&I

F&I matters because of how it is measured. Dealers track it as its own department, and benchmark studies report it with its own figures. Deloitte Australia's 2025 dealership benchmarks, for example, report F&I selling gross per retailed vehicle as a separate measure next to new and used vehicle gross profit and parts and service margins. That study covers Australian dealers, so its figures are not a measure of U.S. results.

The same measure shows up in company filings. In the business strategy section of its 10-K, AutoNation refers to "industry-leading finance and insurance gross profit per vehicle retailed" without giving a figure in that passage. The point for a reader is that F&I gross profit per vehicle retailed is a standard way for dealers to describe this line, so it is worth knowing what the phrase means: the gross profit from F&I divided by the number of vehicles sold at retail.

Looking at F&I as its own line helps a reader ask a few clear questions:

- How much of the dealership's gross profit comes from F&I compared with vehicle sales and service?
- Is the dealership paid by commission on loans placed with outside lenders, or does it also carry loans itself?
- How does F&I per vehicle sold compare with other dealers in the same market?

None of these questions has a single right answer. They are the questions an analyst asks when reading a dealership's financial statements.

## What to take away

An F&I office arranges financing through third-party lenders and sells protection products. The dealership is generally paid in commissions, and for some products it can also share in later underwriting profit. Large retailers report it as one of four lines, and benchmark studies track it by vehicle retailed.
