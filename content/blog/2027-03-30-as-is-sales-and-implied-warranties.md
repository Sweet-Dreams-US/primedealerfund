Every used car a dealership sells comes with a question that shapes the sale: who pays if something breaks after the customer drives away? The answer sits in two ideas that appear in used vehicle sales across the United States, the "as is" sale and the implied warranty. This lesson explains both in plain terms, using the Federal Trade Commission's own guidance, so you can read a dealership's used car business with a clearer picture of where the risk sits.

## What an "as is" sale means

When a dealer sells a used car "as is," the dealer will not pay for problems or repairs that come up after the sale. The buyer takes on that risk. The car is sold in the condition it is in on the day of the sale, and the dealer makes no promise about what happens next.

That does not mean the dealer says nothing about the car. It means the dealer is not backing the car with a promise to fix it. A customer who buys as is should expect to pay for any repair that follows, even a costly one.

The FTC notes that verbal promises are a common source of trouble. If a salesperson says a repair will be done after the sale, that promise should be written down on the Buyers Guide. Without it, the promise may be hard to enforce later.

## What implied warranties are

An implied warranty is an unwritten promise from the dealer. A used car sold without a written warranty still carries implied warranties, unless the car is sold as is. The FTC describes two of them.

The warranty of merchantability is the promise that the car will run and perform its basic functions. If a buyer claims a breach, the buyer has to show that the defect existed at the time of the sale.

The warranty of fitness for a particular purpose applies when the buyer relies on the dealer's advice that a vehicle suits a specific use. A buyer who says a car must tow a trailer, and who buys it because the dealer said it would, is relying on that advice.

How long an implied warranty lasts depends on state law. The FTC says it can last as long as four years in some places.

## The FTC Buyers Guide

The FTC's Used Car Rule requires a dealer to post a Buyers Guide in every used car it offers for sale. The FTC's business guidance says the rule covers dealers who sell more than five used vehicles in a 12 month period. It applies in every state except Maine and Wisconsin, which use their own version, and it also covers Washington, D.C., Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands and American Samoa.

The Guide has to be posted before the car is displayed for sale or before a customer can inspect it for purchase. It must be in plain view with both sides visible. A Guide left in the glove box, the trunk or under a seat does not comply. It can come off for a test drive, but it goes back on when the drive ends.

The Guide has several boxes. The ones that matter for this lesson are:

- "As Is, No Dealer Warranty," which the dealer checks when state law allows it and the dealer offers no written or implied warranty.
- "Implied Warranties Only," which the dealer checks in states that limit or prohibit eliminating implied warranties, when the dealer offers no written warranty.
- "Warranty," which the dealer checks when it offers an express warranty, and then completes the section on what is covered.

:::note The Guide overrides the contract
The FTC says the Buyers Guide will override anything in the sales contract. If the dealer and buyer negotiate a change to the warranty, the change has to appear on the Guide, and the buyer must receive the final version at the sale.
:::

## Where state law changes the answer

The federal rule decides what has to be disclosed. State law decides a good deal of what the disclosure means. In some states, checking the "As Is" box can legally eliminate implied warranties. In others, an as is sale requires specific language or a separate document. Some states do not let a dealer remove implied warranties at all, which is why the "Implied Warranties Only" box exists.

Many states also have laws similar to the Used Car Rule, and state and local officials can enforce them. The FTC tells buyers to ask their state attorney general which disclosures apply where they live. For anyone studying a dealership group that operates across several states, this is a reminder that one federal form can lead to different legal results from one state to the next.

## Service contracts and the 90 day rule

A service contract is a separate product the buyer pays for, and the FTC treats it differently from a dealer warranty. If the buyer must pay to get coverage, it is a service contract, and the dealer cannot check the "Warranty" box for it. The Guide has its own box for service contracts.

There is one rule with real consequences for a dealer. According to the FTC, a service contract bought within 90 days of a used car purchase prevents the dealer from removing implied warranties on the systems that contract covers. A dealer that sells a service contract alongside an as is car therefore cannot count on the as is box to remove implied warranties on those systems.

## Why this matters when you read a dealership's business

For a dealership, the Buyers Guide is not paperwork to finish after the sale. It is the record of who carries repair risk on each used car. Mistakes are costly. The FTC says dealers who violate the rule may face penalties of up to $53,088 per violation in an enforcement action, and notes that the figure is adjusted for inflation over time.

For someone learning how dealerships earn money, the lesson is simple. The way a store handles used car warranties affects its repair costs, its service department traffic and its exposure to disputes. Those are operating details that sit behind the revenue lines.
