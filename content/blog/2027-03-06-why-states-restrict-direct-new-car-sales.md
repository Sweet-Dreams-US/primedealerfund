Most people who buy a new car in the United States walk into a store owned by a local business, not by the company that built the car. That is not an accident of habit. In nearly every state it is the law. Understanding why helps explain how the auto retail business is organized, and why a franchised dealership is a different kind of business from a store a manufacturer runs itself.

## How new cars started out being sold

In the early days of the auto industry, manufacturers sold directly to customers in several ways. Cars were sold from factories, through the mail, in department stores, and by traveling representatives. There was no single standard way to get a car to a buyer.

During the 1930s, manufacturers began leaning on independent franchised dealerships to sell and service their vehicles. A franchised dealer is a separately owned business that signs an agreement with a manufacturer to sell that brand's new vehicles. The dealer puts up the land, the building, the inventory financing and the staff. The manufacturer builds the cars and supplies them to the dealer.

That arrangement shaped the industry that exists today. A manufacturer could grow its sales network without paying to build every store, and local owners took on the risk of running them.

## Why laws followed

Once dealers had invested in showrooms, service bays and inventory, they had a clear worry. A manufacturer could open its own stores nearby and compete against the same dealers it had signed up. The manufacturer controls the supply of cars and the price it charges the dealer, so a dealer would struggle to compete with it.

Laws restricting direct sales emerged in the 1930s era to address that worry. The stated rationale was to shield dealers from competing with manufacturer-owned stores. According to the Wikipedia summary of the topic, by 2010 all 50 states had laws barring direct sales by manufacturers that had franchised dealer networks. The same source says these laws have been actively defended into the 21st century and apply to online sales made within a state.

:::note Worth knowing
Franchise regulation grew over time. A Mercatus Center analysis reports that in 1979 fewer than half of states regulated all three of licensing, termination and exclusive territories. By 2014 Maryland was the only state not regulating all three, and it regulated two of them.
:::

## What the laws usually cover

State dealer franchise laws are not only about direct sales. They commonly deal with three areas:

- Licensing, which controls who may sell new vehicles in the state.
- Termination, which limits when a manufacturer can end a dealer's franchise.
- Exclusive territories, which affect where a manufacturer can place additional dealers of the same brand.

The direct sales restriction is one piece of that larger body of law. In plain terms, if a manufacturer has franchised dealers in a state, it generally cannot sell new vehicles to the public in that state through its own stores.

## Why Tesla made the topic well known

For many years, the franchise system raised few questions outside the industry. That changed when Tesla began selling mass-produced electric vehicles in the 2010s and chose not to use franchised dealers. Tesla argued that dealers would not properly explain the advantages of electric vehicles. Elon Musk also said a dealer network would create a conflict of interest, since dealers earn most of their sales and service income from gasoline vehicles.

Tesla runs galleries in city centers where cars can be viewed but are ordered online. In states that ban direct sales, it treats the sale as an out-of-state transaction and ships the car in. It also argues that franchise laws do not apply to it, since it never had franchised dealers. Dealer associations in several states have sued to stop it.

Different states landed in different places. The Wikipedia table sorts them into groups. Some states have no exemption at all and ban direct sales outright. Some allow direct sales by manufacturers that do not have franchise agreements. Some allow it only for makers of electric vehicles exclusively. Others have provisions that, in practice, benefit only Tesla. A few have no specific prohibition.

Later entrants raised the same issue. Rivian and Lucid won a lawsuit in Illinois in 2022 and were granted dealer licenses there. The same article describes a 2025 lawsuit by the National Automobile Dealers Association against Scout Motors, a Volkswagen subsidiary that plans direct sales.

## The case for the laws

Supporters of franchise laws make several points. A 2014 report by Maryann Keller and Kenneth Elias, prepared for the National Automobile Dealers Association, argued that franchised dealers offer better prices through competition among dealers selling the same brand. The report also said dealers advocate for customers in warranty and recall disputes, and that they create local jobs and tax revenue.

The Mercatus analysis also lists points in favor of some of the rules. Dealer termination rules can help struggling dealerships, and manufacturers sometimes voluntarily offer exclusive territories to encourage dealers to invest in promoting their products.

## The case against

Critics argue the opposite. They say the laws suppress competition that would benefit consumers, and some call them protectionism. The Wikipedia article states that the Federal Trade Commission recommends allowing direct manufacturer sales, and that a 2000 Goldman Sachs analyst report projected average savings of $2,225 on a $26,000 car. The Mercatus authors argue that rules blocking dealer network efficiency likely cost car buyers, and that mandatory exclusive territories can insulate dealers from competition.

The Mercatus authors also say they do not claim to know the best way of organizing auto distribution. Their argument is that mandatory franchise laws prevent manufacturers and consumers from finding out.

:::note Worth knowing
Both sides cite studies and both sides have a point to prove. When you read research on this topic, check who commissioned it. The dealer association report and the economists' critiques reach different conclusions from the same market.
:::

## What this means for people who study dealerships
