Used cars are a large part of what a dealership sells, and the way a buyer checks one tells you a lot about how the retail side of the business works. One of the most common checks is a vehicle history report. It is easy to get and easy to read, so many shoppers treat a clean report as proof that the car is sound. The Federal Trade Commission says plainly that this is a mistake: "A vehicle history report is not a substitute for an independent vehicle inspection."

This article explains what each tool is for, where the report stops, and how a buyer can use both to make a better decision.

## What a vehicle history report is for

A history report is a record of what has happened to a specific car over its life. It is tied to the vehicle identification number, or VIN. A buyer looks at it to learn about the car's past before deciding whether to look at its present condition.

The FTC's Buyers Guide, which dealers must display on every used car they offer, tells shoppers to get a history report. So the report is a recommended step. It is a useful first screen. The problem starts when it is treated as the last step.

## What a report can show, and what it usually does not

According to the FTC, a history report can show things like accidents and flood damage. It typically will not reveal mechanical problems.

That limit makes sense once you think about what a report is. It is a history, a list of events that were recorded about the car. It does not describe the car as it sits in front of you today. Worn brakes, a leaking seal, a transmission that is starting to slip, or tires that need replacing are conditions of the car right now. A report built from past events is not designed to describe them.

A clean report therefore tells a buyer that certain recorded problems did not show up. It does not tell the buyer that the car is mechanically healthy.

:::note What the FTC says
The FTC states that a vehicle history report is not a substitute for an independent vehicle inspection, and that reports typically do not reveal mechanical problems.
:::

## What an independent inspection adds

The FTC advises buyers to pay an independent mechanic to do a mechanical inspection of a used car. This applies even if the dealer has certified the car, or if it comes with a warranty or a service contract.

The buyer pays the inspection fee. The FTC's view is that the fee can help a buyer avoid a car with major problems. A mechanic looks at the vehicle as it is today, which is the question a history report cannot answer.

The FTC also suggests asking the mechanic for a written report. The report should include the make, model, and VIN, and it should list the repairs the car needs with cost estimates. Those estimates are useful in two ways. They show the buyer what the car may cost to own in the near term, and they can be used to negotiate the price.

## A mechanical inspection is not a safety inspection

The two terms are easy to mix up. The FTC explains that a safety inspection generally covers only conditions that make a car unsafe to drive. A mechanical inspection is broader. It is meant to find problems a buyer would want to know about before paying for the car, including ones that do not yet make the car unsafe.

When a seller says a car "passed inspection," a buyer should ask which kind of inspection was done and who did it. A safety check does not answer the same questions as a mechanic's review of the engine, transmission, and other major systems.

## If the dealer will not let the car leave the lot

Sometimes a buyer cannot drive a car to a shop. The FTC offers two options. The first is to look for a mobile inspection service that will come to the dealership. The second is to ask the dealer to bring the car to a facility the buyer chooses.

The FTC also gives a plain rule for the case where a dealer refuses any independent inspection: consider another dealer. A seller who is confident in a car usually has little reason to block a mechanic from looking at it.

## The Buyers Guide and getting promises in writing

Federal rules require dealers to display a Buyers Guide on every used car they offer for sale, and to give it to the buyer after the sale. The FTC says this includes light-duty vans and trucks, demonstrators, and program cars. Motorcycles and most recreational vehicles are generally exempt. A buyer is also entitled to a Guide when buying a used car online. Maine and Wisconsin dealers use their own versions.

The Guide lists the major mechanical and electrical systems and their common problems. It states whether the car is sold "as is" or with a warranty, and what share of repair costs the dealer will pay. The FTC notes that the Guide overrides the sales contract, and that it must show any changes a buyer negotiates in the warranty coverage. Because of that, anything negotiated should appear on both the Guide and the contract.

The FTC also warns that verbal assurances are hard to enforce. If a salesperson promises something about the car, the buyer should get it in writing.

:::note Why paper matters
Per the FTC, the Buyers Guide overrides the sales contract, so negotiated warranty changes should be written on both documents.
:::

## Putting the steps in order

A sensible sequence for a used car purchase uses each tool for what it does well.

First, get the history report and read it for recorded accidents or flood damage. Second, read the Buyers Guide to see whether the car is sold as is or with a warranty. Third, pay an independent mechanic for a mechanical inspection and ask for a written report with repair estimates. Fourth, use that report to decide whether to buy and what price is fair. Fifth, put every promise in writing and make sure negotiated changes appear on the Buyers Guide and the contract.

The history report is the cheapest step and the inspection is the one that looks at the actual car. Skipping the second because the first looked clean is the mistake the FTC warns about.

## Why this matters beyond one purchase

How a dealership handles used cars is part of how its retail business runs. Clear paperwork, a Buyers Guide on every car, and willingness to let a buyer bring in a mechanic are all signs of a dealership that follows the rules and respects the customer. Understanding the basics of the used car sale helps anyone who is learning how dealerships operate.
