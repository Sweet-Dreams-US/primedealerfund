When you read about a dealership sale, the headline price rarely matches the value of the lot, the building and the cars on it. Part of the price pays for things you cannot touch. Those things are the dealer's goodwill and the right to sell a manufacturer's vehicles. This article explains what those two items are, how accountants and valuation firms put a number on them, and why that number can look different from one document to the next.

It is general information about how dealerships are valued. It is not investment advice.

## Two names for one idea

Dealers call the intangible part of a store's value "blue sky." Accountants call a related piece of it goodwill. A dealer-focused accounting article from EisnerAmper says goodwill is "also known as" blue sky and describes it as an intangible asset that comes from relationships with repeat buyers and a reputation for fair prices and service.

Kerrigan Advisors, an advisory firm for dealerships, describes blue sky as the store's intangible value, including goodwill. A common way to value a store, the firm says, is to add the store's material assets to its blue sky value. Material assets are the things you can count, such as inventory, equipment and real estate. Blue sky is what is left over.

## Where the franchise fits

A dealership does not simply own the right to sell a brand. Forvis Mazars, an accounting firm, explains that franchise rights are the ability to sell and service vehicles in an area of responsibility, as outlined in the franchise agreement with the manufacturer. A manufacturer first grants those rights through what the industry calls an open point application.

The firm calls blue sky the most subjective asset in a dealership, because it is the entire bucket of intangibles. For purchase accounting under U.S. accounting rules (GAAP), that bucket has to be split. Part of it is goodwill. Part of it is identifiable intangibles, and franchise rights are the identifiable intangible that frequently has the most value.

:::note Worth knowing
Forvis Mazars says each franchise agreement typically has to be valued separately for GAAP purposes, not on a combined basis. A store with more than one brand can therefore carry more than one franchise value.
:::

## How the number is built on paper

When a buyer acquires a dealership, the purchase price is spread across what was acquired. EisnerAmper explains that the buyer assigns the price to assets and liabilities at fair value. Whatever price is not assigned to an identifiable asset or liability is recorded as goodwill. The article adds that other assets, including franchise agreements, may need an outside appraisal.

Forvis Mazars lists three approaches that valuation specialists use for franchise rights: the asset approach, the market approach and the income approach.

The asset approach asks what it would cost to obtain an open point. The article says it is seldom used. The market approach applies a market multiple to normalized earnings before taxes and before LIFO adjustments. A narrower version, which the firm calls the modified market approach, limits those earnings to the ones attributable to the franchise. That typically means all new vehicle earnings plus part of the earnings from used vehicles, service, parts and finance and insurance. The income approach, which the firm calls a franchise earnings approach, values the earnings the franchise is expected to produce over several periods.

## Why the price is a multiple of earnings

Blue sky is usually quoted as a multiple of earnings. Kerrigan Advisors gives an example using data from Haig Partners for 2025: Kia stores averaged 4.5 to 6 times earnings, and Nissan stores averaged 3 to 4 times earnings. Buyers, the article says, covet some franchises more than others, and that is why the multiples differ by brand.

The same article gives a worked example for a hypothetical group. It has $1.5 million in earnings and $1.5 million in material assets, and a combined multiple of 4. Multiplying earnings by 4 gives $6 million of blue sky. Adding the $1.5 million of assets gives a raw value of $7.5 million. The article calls this a raw value, a starting point rather than a final price.

## What moves the multiple

Kerrigan Advisors names three influences on the multiple: the store's earnings, the growth potential of the local market population, and the store's real estate expenses. The article's example also gives a lower multiple to a store with stable but low-growth earnings that needs renovation.

Forvis Mazars adds that the type of franchise a dealership holds is a significant component of its value, and that state franchise laws protect the franchise agreement. Because the franchise sits at the center of the value, the brand a store holds matters a great deal to its number on paper.

:::note Worth knowing
EisnerAmper says goodwill is tested for impairment at least once a year under GAAP, and that impairment occurs when fair value falls below book value. Its 2015 article also describes an alternative for private companies, from FASB guidance at that time, that allows goodwill to be amortized over 10 years or less and tested only when a triggering event occurs. Examples it gives include unanticipated competition, the loss of a key person and a new adverse regulation. Accounting rules change, so check the current standard before relying on that detail.
:::

## Why book value and sale price differ

Taken together, the three sources show why two numbers for the same store can both be correct. A balance sheet records goodwill and franchise rights from a past purchase, at the fair values assigned then. A buyer or seller today prices the store off current earnings and a current multiple. A store whose earnings have risen since the last purchase can be worth more than its recorded intangibles. A store whose earnings have fallen can be worth less, and that is when impairment tests matter.

Neither number is a promise about the future. Valuation firms say plainly that blue sky is subjective, and the range of multiples in the Kerrigan example shows how much a brand and a local market can change the result.

## What to take from this

If you read a dealership financial statement, look for how the intangibles are recorded and when they were last valued. If you read a sale price, ask how much of it is material assets and how much is blue sky. Then ask what earnings and what multiple produced the blue sky figure, and whether the franchise rights were valued by brand.

This article is general information about dealership valuation and not investment advice. Talk with a qualified accountant or advisor about any decision of your own.
