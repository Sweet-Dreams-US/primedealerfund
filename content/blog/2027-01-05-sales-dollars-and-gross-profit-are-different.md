When people talk about how big a car dealership is, they usually talk about sales. How many vehicles did it sell, and how many dollars came in? Those numbers matter. But they do not tell you how much the dealership kept after paying for what it sold. For that you need a second measure, gross profit. The two measures can point in very different directions, and understanding the gap is a basic skill in auto retail.

This post explains the difference and then uses the 2025 results of one public retailer to show it with real numbers.

## Two measures, two questions

Sales dollars, also called revenue, answer the question: how much money came in from selling this? Gross profit answers a different question: how much of that money was left after the direct cost of what was sold?

A simple example shows the idea. If a dealership sells a vehicle for $40,000 that it paid $38,000 to acquire, the sale adds $40,000 to revenue and $2,000 to gross profit. Gross margin is the gross profit divided by the revenue, which here is 5 percent. A line of business with big revenue can have a thin margin. A smaller line can have a thick one.

Gross profit is not the same as net profit. It comes before the rent, payroll, utilities, and other costs of running the store. It tells you how much each line contributes toward paying those costs.


## How big dealership sales are

The National Automobile Dealers Association reports the scale of the industry in its 2025 data report. Total dealership sales were about $1.30 trillion in 2025, which works out to about $76.6 million per dealership. New vehicles made up 54.9 percent of sales dollars, used vehicles 31.8 percent, and service and parts 13.3 percent.

:::note Worth knowing
According to the NADA 2025 data report, new vehicles were 54.9 percent of total dealership sales dollars in 2025, used vehicles were 31.8 percent, and service and parts were 13.3 percent.
:::

On sales alone, new vehicles look like the center of the business. The average new vehicle retail selling price was $48,205, and the average dealership sold 955 new vehicles. Those figures make new vehicle sales the largest source of dollars coming in.

## What gross profit looked like at one large retailer

The NADA split above is by sales dollars. To see gross profit by line, we can look at AutoNation, a public retailer that reports each line separately. These are its full-year 2025 results, in millions of dollars:

- New vehicle: $13,501.3 revenue, $664.8 gross profit, 4.9 percent margin.
- Used vehicle: $7,814.0 revenue, $462.6 gross profit, 5.9 percent margin.
- Parts and service: $4,835.4 revenue, $2,355.1 gross profit, 48.7 percent margin.
- Finance and insurance: $1,464.4 revenue and gross profit, reported net.
- Total: $27,631.4 revenue, $4,948.5 gross profit, 17.9 percent margin.

New vehicles were about 49 percent of the revenue at AutoNation but only about 13 percent of its gross profit. Parts and service were about 17.5 percent of revenue but about 48 percent of gross profit. Finance and insurance were about 5 percent of revenue and about 30 percent of gross profit.

:::note Worth knowing
In the 2025 results of AutoNation, new vehicles produced about 49 percent of revenue and about 13 percent of gross profit, while parts and service produced about 17.5 percent of revenue and about 48 percent of gross profit.
:::


## Why the shares differ

The gap comes from how each line is priced. A vehicle has a large purchase price, so even a small margin produces a large revenue number. A repair or a maintenance visit has a much smaller price but a much higher margin, so a modest revenue number turns into a large share of gross profit.

Finance and insurance work differently again. AutoNation reports them net, so revenue and gross profit are the same number. There is no vehicle cost to subtract, which is why the line shows a 100 percent margin and a large share of gross profit from a small share of revenue.

Per unit, the same pattern shows up. AutoNation reported gross profit of $2,564 per new vehicle retailed and $1,555 per retail used vehicle, along with $2,769 per vehicle in finance and insurance.

## What not to conclude

These numbers describe one company in one year. They do not describe every dealership. A rural franchise store, a luxury store, and a large public group can all have different mixes. Brand, location, and the number of vehicles a store services after the sale all change the picture.

It would also be a mistake to read the table as saying that vehicle sales do not matter. Selling a vehicle is often what creates a later service customer and a finance and insurance opportunity. The lines are connected. The point is only that revenue and gross profit should not be treated as the same thing.

This article is general education about how dealership accounting works. It is not investment advice and it does not describe the results of any particular investment.

## Questions to ask when you read dealership numbers

When you see the results of a dealership, a few questions help. Which measure is being shown, revenue or gross profit? Is the figure for one line or for the whole store? Is the margin shown, and is it before or after the costs of running the store? Is the time period the same in every number being compared?

If the answers are not clear, the number is hard to use. Revenue by itself says how busy the store is. Gross profit by line says where the money comes from.

## A short summary

Sales dollars show how much money came in. Gross profit shows how much was left after the direct cost of the sale. In the 2025 results of AutoNation, new vehicles brought in about 49 percent of revenue and about 13 percent of gross profit, while parts and service brought in about 17.5 percent of revenue and about 48 percent of gross profit. Looking at both measures gives a more accurate picture of a dealership than looking at either one alone.
