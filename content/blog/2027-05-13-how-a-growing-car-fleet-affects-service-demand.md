A dealership sells cars, but it also keeps cars running after the sale. That second job is the service and parts department, and its workload depends on a number most people never look up: how many vehicles are on the road and how old they are. This article explains those two ideas, shows what the latest data says, and describes why investors who study dealerships pay attention to them.

## What "vehicles in operation" means

Vehicles in operation is the count of registered cars, trucks and SUVs that are on the road at a given time. It is different from new vehicle sales. Sales tell you how many vehicles entered the market in a year. Vehicles in operation tells you how many vehicles exist that could need an oil change, new brakes or a repair.

Every vehicle sold stays in the count until it is scrapped or leaves the market. Because most vehicles last many years, the count grows whenever fewer vehicles are retired than are sold. A large fleet means a large pool of possible service work for the shops that serve it.

## Why the age of the fleet matters

Age matters because wear is not constant over a vehicle's life. A newer vehicle usually needs routine maintenance such as fluid changes, tire rotations and filters. As a vehicle ages, parts wear out. Brakes, batteries, suspension components, belts and cooling parts need replacement. The work is more frequent and often more costly per visit.

So two fleets of the same size can create different amounts of service demand. A fleet that skews older needs more repairs and replacement parts than a fleet that skews newer. That is the reason analysts track average vehicle age along with the total count.

## What the latest data says

S&P Global Mobility reported that the average age of vehicles in the United States rose to 12.8 years in 2025. The same report puts the light vehicle fleet at 289 million vehicles. The report expects a wave of service demand to reach repair shops, parts suppliers and service providers, and it notes that vehicles in the range of six to 14 years old need more regular servicing, repairs and part replacements.

:::note Worth knowing
S&P Global Mobility reported an average vehicle age of 12.8 years in 2025 across a fleet of 289 million light vehicles in the United States.
:::

Two facts stand out. The fleet is large, and the average vehicle is well into the age range where parts need replacing. Both point toward steady demand for maintenance and repair work.

## How this connects to dealership service departments

Dealerships earn money in more than one way. They sell new vehicles, sell used vehicles, arrange financing and insurance products, and run service and parts operations. The National Automobile Dealers Association publishes data on these areas for its members and for the public.

NADA reported dealership service and parts sales exceeding $164 billion for the full year 2025. Its mid-year report for the first half of 2026 cites service and parts sales of nearly $83 billion.

:::note Worth knowing
NADA data lists dealership service and parts sales above $164 billion for 2025, and nearly $83 billion for the first half of 2026.
:::

Service and parts work is tied to the vehicles already on the road, not to this month's showroom traffic. That is why people who study dealerships look at fleet size and age when they ask how much service work a dealership could see over time. A store that serves a large, older fleet in its area has more potential service demand than a store in a smaller or newer market. A dealership still has to earn that work. Customers can choose an independent shop, and a service department has to be staffed, equipped and priced well to keep customers coming back.

## What fleet data does not tell you

Fleet data describes a market. It does not describe any one dealership or any one investment. A few limits are worth keeping in mind.

First, national averages hide local differences. The average age in one town can be far from the national figure. Second, a larger pool of older vehicles does not guarantee that a particular dealership captures the work. Third, trends can change. Vehicle designs, ownership patterns and repair needs shift over time, and a figure published for one year may not hold for the next.

For these reasons, fleet data is one input among many. It helps explain why service and parts is a recurring part of dealership operations. It does not predict results for a specific store.

## How to read fleet data yourself

You can check the same figures that analysts use. Start with the S&P Global Mobility report on average vehicle age and note the year, the average age and the fleet size. Then open the NADA data pages and look at the service and parts figures for the most recent full year and the most recent mid-year report. Compare them over time and look for direction, not a single number.

When you read any figure, check who published it, what year it covers and what it counts. A vehicle count from one source and a sales figure from another may use different definitions, so compare them with care.
