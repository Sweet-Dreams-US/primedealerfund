A car dealership does not run like most other retail businesses. A dealer sells vehicles under a franchise agreement with a manufacturer, and that agreement controls which brand the dealer sells and where. Because the manufacturer holds so much power in that relationship, state legislatures have written rules that limit what a manufacturer can do. Two of the most important limits cover closing a dealership and opening a new one close by.

This article explains how those limits generally work. It is written for readers who want to understand the auto retail industry, including people who are considering an investment that involves dealerships.

## Why states regulate the dealer and manufacturer relationship

The Congressional Research Service reports that every state has enacted a dealer franchise law. Alaska was the last state to pass one, in 2002. The report describes these laws as an effort to level the playing field between dealers and manufacturers.

The National Automobile Dealers Association has described the purpose in similar terms, pointing to what it calls "the vast disparity in the bargaining position between a dealer and its manufacturer." A dealer often invests heavily in a facility, inventory, and staff that are tied to one brand. If the manufacturer could end the agreement at will, that investment could lose its value overnight.

:::note Every state has a dealer franchise law
The Congressional Research Service reports that all states have a dealer franchise law, and that courts have upheld these state laws. The rules differ from state to state, so the details in any one state matter.
:::

## Limits on terminating a dealer

The most common protection is a good cause requirement. According to the Congressional Research Service, typical state laws can require "a manufacturer to show good cause in order to terminate a dealer agreement." In practice, this means a manufacturer cannot simply decide it would like a different dealer in that location. It needs a reason that the law accepts, and the dealer usually gets notice and a chance to respond.

Some of these laws go further. They can set a method for working out the fair value of a franchise that is terminated, and they can set basic rules for passing a franchise from one generation to the next.

The federal government also has a law on the subject. The Automobile Dealers' Day in Court Act of 1956 gives dealers a legal remedy when a manufacturer does not act in good faith in carrying out a franchise agreement, or in terminating, canceling, or not renewing it. The Congressional Research Service notes that dealers say this federal law has been ineffective. It cites an industry study that found dealers obtained relief in about 20 of 115 cases filed in the law's first 30 years. That is one reason the state laws carry most of the weight.

## Limits on adding or moving a dealership

The second major limit protects an existing dealer from a new competitor of the same brand. Many state laws define a relevant market area, which the Congressional Research Service describes as "the geographic area within which a new-car dealer is presumed to be directly competing with existing dealers." A manufacturer that wants to add or relocate a same-brand dealership in that area must follow a set process.

Minnesota's statute shows how this can look. The relevant market area is a ten-mile radius around an existing dealership. Before adding or relocating a same-brand dealership there, the manufacturer must notify each same-brand dealer in writing. A dealer then has 30 days to sue, and the manufacturer may not proceed unless a court finds good cause. Approving an additional sales, service, or parts location counts as establishing a dealership.

Indiana's statute follows a similar pattern with a different forum. A franchisor must give written notice to each same-brand dealer in the relevant market area. A dealer that receives notice can file a declaratory judgment action with the state division within thirty days. While that action is pending, the franchisor "may not establish or relocate the proposed new motor vehicle dealer until the division has rendered a decision." The version of the Indiana code reviewed for this article notes that it may not be the most current, so anyone relying on it should check the official Indiana sources.

:::note Notice comes first
In both Minnesota and Indiana, the manufacturer must give written notice to the nearby same-brand dealers before a new or relocated dealership can go ahead. Each dealer then has 30 days to challenge it.
:::

## What counts as good cause

Good cause is not left to guesswork. The statutes list factors that a court or agency weighs. In Minnesota, the factors include the investment of the proposed and existing dealers, the effect on retail business and consumers, the adequacy of competition, service, and facilities, population and registration trends, and the stability of existing franchises. The Minnesota statute also says good cause cannot rest solely on a desire for further market penetration.

Indiana's list is similar. It includes the permanence of the investment, the effect on retail sales and consumers, whether the change helps or harms public welfare, whether existing dealers already provide adequate competition and service, and population and registration growth or decline in the market area.

These lists tell you what a manufacturer has to be ready to show. They also tell you what an existing dealer can argue: that the market is already well served, that its own investment is substantial, or that a new location would harm customers.

## Exceptions for small moves

The rules do not apply to every change. Both states carve out short moves. Under the Indiana statute, a relocation of no more than two miles from the current location is exempt. Reopening or replacing a dealership that closed within the previous 365 days is also exempt if the new location is within two miles of the closed one. Minnesota has a comparable exception for a good faith reopening within two miles of a former same-brand dealership that operated in the previous two years, and for a relocation within five miles of the existing site that is not within five miles of another same-brand dealer.

## Why the rules are debated

These laws are not universally praised. The Congressional Research Service reports that dealers say they need protection from termination without cause. Automakers, on the other hand, assert that some state franchise laws make it difficult and costly for them to adjust to changing market conditions. Some experts argue that termination provisions are a key reason for the existence of too many dealers.

For anyone studying auto retail, the practical takeaway is that a dealership's franchise is not just a contract. It sits inside a legal framework that makes it harder for a manufacturer to close the store or put a new competitor next door. How strong that framework is depends on the state.

## Questions worth asking about any dealership

When you evaluate a dealership or a business that owns dealerships, a few questions follow from this lesson. Which brands does the dealership sell, and under what franchise agreements? Which state's franchise law applies? What notice and good cause rules protect the store from termination? How many same-brand dealers are nearby, and how would a new or relocated dealer be handled under local rules?

The answers will not appear in a brochure. They come from the franchise agreement itself and from the statutes of the state where the dealership operates. Reading both is the only way to understand the protections that are in place.
