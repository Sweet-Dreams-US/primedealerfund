When people picture a car dealership, they often see one brand's name over the door. Many dealers work differently. A multibrand dealer sells vehicles from more than one automaker. This article explains what that means, how franchise rules shape what a dealer can carry, where a dealership earns its money, and which consumer rules apply to the used cars on the lot.

## What a multibrand dealer is

Multibrand dealers, also called multi-maker dealers, sell cars from different, independent carmakers. Some of them specialize, for example in electric vehicles. That sets them apart from the more familiar arrangement, where a dealer sells under a franchise agreement with a single automaker or its authorized distributor.

The difference shows up in what a shopper can compare on one lot. A store tied to one automaker shows that maker's lineup. A multibrand store can put vehicles from several makers side by side.

## Why franchise law shapes the lot

In the United States, most states bar automakers from selling new cars directly to consumers. By 2010, all 50 states and the District of Columbia had laws of this kind. New cars go through licensed, bonded, independently owned dealers instead.

Franchise rules also protect the dealer. A franchise can generally be withdrawn only for illegal activity, and most states require the manufacturer to buy back inventory if a dealership is terminated. Most states also restrict new dealerships that would compete with existing ones, which has made some dealerships effectively hereditary.

Europe took a different route on brands. In 2006 the European Commission ruled that it was anti-competitive for manufacturers to prohibit dealers from carrying multiple brands. In the United Kingdom, most dealerships now sell multiple brands through franchise networks. The point for readers in the United States is that the number of brands a dealer carries is a legal and contractual matter, and it varies by country and by state.

## Single stores and dealer groups

Many modern dealerships belong to corporate-owned chains with hundreds of locations. In the United Kingdom, groups such as Pendragon, Lookers, and Arnold Clark account for a significant share of national vehicle sales.

A group is the owner of its locations. The brands a particular location carries depend on the agreements that location holds. When you read about a dealer group, it helps to separate two questions: who owns the stores, and which brands each store sells.

## Where a dealership earns money

Dealerships have more than one source of income, and they are not equal. Using US figures, the Car dealership article on Wikipedia says new cars bring little profit. Used cars are a source of profit, though a smaller one than service. Service is the main profit source in the United States. Spare parts sales and repairs count as after-sales revenue, and financing and insurance are listed among after-sales services.

:::note Worth knowing
Wikipedia reports that selling new cars earns little profit at US dealerships, and that service is the main profit source.
:::

Prime Dealer Fund's site describes each dealership as having seven distinct profit centers, including new and used sales, finance and insurance, service, parts, and, at some locations, a body shop. A multibrand store has the same profit centers as any other store. What changes is the mix of vehicles that feeds them.

## The rules for used cars on any lot

Whatever brands a dealer carries, the same federal consumer rules apply to the used cars it sells. The Federal Trade Commission explains the main points.

A dealer must display a Buyers Guide on every used car it offers for sale and give it to the buyer after the sale. This covers light-duty vans and trucks, demonstrators, and program cars. The Guide shows whether the car is sold "as is" or with a warranty, and what percentage of repair costs the dealer will pay under that warranty. The Guide overrides the sales contract.

A car sold "as is" with no dealer warranty means the dealer will not pay for repairs after the sale, and the buyer takes on that risk. If a car has no written warranty and is not sold as is, implied warranties apply. These are unwritten promises, such as the promise that the car will do its basic functions.

A service contract is not a warranty, even when it is called an extended warranty. Federal law does not give buyers a three-day right to cancel. Some states require dealers to offer one, and in others a return option exists only if the dealer chooses to offer it.

:::note Worth knowing
The FTC says a vehicle history report does not reveal mechanical problems, so it recommends an independent mechanical inspection, even for certified cars.
:::

## Questions worth asking at a multibrand dealer

A few practical questions follow from the points above.

- Is the vehicle new, or is it a used vehicle? New vehicles come from a brand the dealer is franchised for. Used vehicles can be of any make.
- Can I see the Buyers Guide on this used car before I decide?
- If the salesperson promised something, is it written on the Guide or in the contract? The FTC notes that spoken promises are difficult to enforce.
- Can I have the car inspected by an independent mechanic?
