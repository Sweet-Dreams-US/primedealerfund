Investors who study auto dealerships often start with a simple question. A dealership sells vehicles, and it also services and repairs them. Do those two businesses move together, or does one of them hold up better when the other slows? This post lays out what the public industry data says, what it does not say, and why the question matters when you look at a dealership as a business.

## Two ways a dealership earns

A franchised dealership has several departments. The ones most people know are new vehicle sales and used vehicle sales. The service and parts department is the other major one, and the industry often calls it fixed operations. In the National Automobile Dealers Association data used here, the department is reported as service and parts, and in the mid-year report it also includes the body shop.

Vehicle sales depend on a customer deciding to buy a vehicle. Service work depends on a customer who already owns one and needs it maintained or repaired. That difference in what drives each department is the reason investors compare them. It is a reason to look at the numbers, though, not a result by itself.

## How large each department is

NADA reports each department's share of total dealership sales dollars. For the first half of 2026, the mid-year report shows new vehicles at 53.6 percent, used vehicles at 32.8 percent, and service, parts and body shop at 13.6 percent. For the first half of 2025 the same figures were 54.2 percent, 32.6 percent and 13.2 percent.

So by sales dollars, service and parts is the smallest of the three. The industry totals give a sense of scale. NADA says franchised light-vehicle dealership sales topped $1.3 trillion in 2025, with service and parts sales exceeding $164 billion. For the first half of 2026 it reports total sales topping $640 billion, with service and parts sales of nearly $83 billion.

:::note Sales dollars are not profit
These shares measure sales dollars. They do not show how much profit each department earns. The NADA reports cited in this post do not break out gross profit by department, so this post does not state a profit share for service and parts.
:::

## How service and parts sales have moved

The mid-year report shows service and parts sales for the first six months of the year rising from about $51.94 billion in 2020 to about $82.99 billion in 2026. The 2025 figure is about $81.08 billion, so the 2026 figure is up roughly $1.9 billion from a year earlier.

That is a large increase over six years. It tells you that service and parts sales have grown. It does not tell you how service and parts would behave in a recession, because the report's figures start in 2020 and do not include earlier downturns.

## Margin is a separate question

Sales dollars and margin are different things. An older NADA report, the 2014 annual financial profile, shows a gross profit margin of 46.14 percent on service and parts sales for that year. That is the share of each service and parts sales dollar left after the direct cost of the work and parts. It is one data point from one year, so it should not be read as a current figure.

Taken together, the two measures describe a department that is smaller than vehicle sales in sales dollars and that, in at least one year, kept a large part of each sales dollar as gross profit. An investor who wants to compare profit by department needs gross profit figures for each department, which are not in the reports cited here.

## What fixed absorption measures

One measure dealers use for the service side is the fixed absorption rate. CBT News reports NADA Dealer Academy data showing a national average of 63.9 percent in August 2025, up from 61 percent in August 2024. The rate measures whether service and parts profits cover the dealership's operating expenses. Experts recommend a rate of 100 percent or higher, and some Virginia dealerships passed 105 percent.

A rate at or above 100 percent means that service and parts alone would cover the operating costs, which would leave profit from vehicle sales as additional earnings. The national average is well below that, which tells you how much room there is between the typical dealership and the target.

## What to take from this

Investors look at service and parts because it is tied to vehicles people already own, and the department has grown in sales dollars since 2020. That is a reasonable starting point for study. A firm claim that service revenue is steadier than car sales needs more than a growth chart, though. It needs figures from years when vehicle sales fell, and it needs gross profit by department. Anyone doing their own research should look for those, and ask any dealership group how it reports its own results by department.
