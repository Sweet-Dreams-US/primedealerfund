When a car comes into a dealership's service department, someone has to pay for the repair. Sometimes that is the vehicle owner. Sometimes it is the vehicle's manufacturer. The difference changes who sets the price, how the dealership gets paid, and how the work shows up in the dealership's results.

For anyone studying dealerships as a business, this is one of the first things to understand about the service side. This article explains the two kinds of work in plain terms.

## The service department is a large business

The National Automobile Dealers Association (NADA) publishes data on the dealership industry. Its 2025 full-year report says dealerships wrote more than 276 million repair orders, with service and parts sales exceeding $164 billion. For the first half of 2026, its mid-year report says dealerships wrote more than 136 million repair orders, with service and parts sales of nearly $83 billion.

A repair order is the written record of a job. Every repair order is paid for by someone, and that is where the split between warranty work and customer-paid work begins.

## What customer-paid work is

Customer-paid work, often called customer pay, is a repair or service the vehicle owner pays for. Think of an owner who brings in a car for a repair that no warranty covers. The dealership sets its own labor rate and parts prices, bills the customer, and collects from the customer.

The labor rate a dealership charges its customers matters beyond customer-pay work, as the next section shows.

## What warranty work is

Warranty work is a repair covered by a manufacturer's warranty. The owner does not pay for it. The dealership does the repair and then asks the manufacturer to pay for the parts and the labor. Recall repairs and other manufacturer-requested repairs work in a similar way.

This makes the manufacturer the dealership's customer for that job. The dealership submits a claim, and the manufacturer reviews it and pays it.

## Why the rate the manufacturer pays matters

State dealer franchise laws deal with this directly. Francis X. O'Brien II of Withum writes that dealer franchise laws in all fifty states require manufacturers or distributors to pay dealers for warranty parts and labor at the dealer's retail customer-pay rates. In other words, the law's goal is that a warranty repair earns about what the same repair would earn if the customer had paid for it.

:::note Worth knowing
Withum reports that manufacturers have applied these laws in widely varying ways, and that this has kept many dealers from receiving full retail rates. The details differ by state.
:::

The mechanics can be technical. Boardman & Clark LLP describes how Wisconsin law works as one example. The statute sets a retail labor rate by dividing a dealer's total retail repair charges by the manufacturer's time allowances for those repairs. Manufacturers usually divide labor charges by the hours the dealer actually billed. Time allowances are often shorter than billed time, so the two methods give different results.

The firm gives a worked example. A repair billed at $100 for 2 hours, with a 1-hour manufacturer allowance, produces a rate of $100 per hour under the statute. Under the manufacturer's usual method it produces $50 per hour, and the manufacturer pays $50 for the 1-hour allowance. This is one state's rule, shown here to explain the idea. Other states write their laws differently.

## Parts are part of the same question

Warranty reimbursement covers parts as well as labor. Boardman & Clark explains that under the Wisconsin statute the parts markup is total parts charges divided by the dealer's total cost for those parts. Withum raises a related concern about recalls. It says manufacturers have reduced the dealer cost they use for recall parts by 80 to 90 percent in some cases, which the author says cancels out the retail reimbursement rate.

For a reader new to the topic, the point is simple. Two repairs that look the same to the person at the service desk can pay the dealership very different amounts, depending on who is paying and which rate applies.

## How the two kinds of work differ

Putting the pieces together, the main differences are these three.

Who pays: the vehicle owner pays for customer-paid work, and the manufacturer pays for warranty work.

Who sets the price: the dealership sets its prices for customer-paid work, while warranty work is paid through the manufacturer's claim process under the rules of the manufacturer and the state.

What can go wrong: a customer can decide a price is too high and go elsewhere, while a manufacturer can reject, reduce, or delay a warranty claim.

## Why this matters when you study a dealership

An investor or analyst looking at a dealership's service department would want to know how much of its work is customer-paid and how much is warranty, because the two depend on different things. Customer-paid work depends on vehicle owners choosing to come back. Warranty work depends on the manufacturer's warranty terms, on the volume of covered repairs, and on how the manufacturer pays its claims.

## The short version

Customer-paid work is billed to the vehicle owner at the dealership's own prices. Warranty work is billed to the manufacturer, and state franchise laws aim to have the manufacturer pay about what the dealership would charge a customer. In practice the rates and the process can be contested, which is why the mix of work matters when you look at a dealership's service business.
