When a carmaker says it will sell cars online at set prices, dealers and the people who invest in dealerships both have reason to pay attention. In March 2021 Volvo made that kind of announcement. This post sticks to what Volvo and others said in public, and how dealers answered. It is general information about auto retail, not a forecast of what any brand will do next.

## What Volvo announced in 2021

Volvo said it planned to become a fully electric car company by 2030. That meant removing combustion engine cars, including hybrids, from its global lineup by the end of the decade. It also said all sales of its pure electric models would move online. Prices and models would be shown on its website, and orders would be placed there.

The showroom was not part of what Volvo said would go away. Physical locations would remain for test drives, and salespeople would help customers place their orders on the website. Volvo said most buyers would probably still work with staff, while some would order on their own. In its words, "the car will still be delivered and demonstrated by our partners."

:::note What Volvo said about cost
Volvo said online sales would show costs transparently. A Volvo executive said margins and costs could fall, and that Volvo and its partners would need to manage that.
:::

## What changed about the 2030 target

Volvo later revised the goal. In 2024 it dropped the plan to be fully electric by 2030. It said it now expects 90 to 100 percent of its 2030 sales to be electrified cars. That mix includes battery electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles, plus a limited number of mild hybrids. Volvo still aims for full electrification and net zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2040, and said "stronger and more stable" government policies are needed to support the transition.

The reports on that revision do not discuss the online sales plan, so this post does not claim to know whether that part changed. The 2021 announcement is still useful because it shows how a manufacturer can describe the dealer's role, and how dealers respond when they read it differently.

## Why dealers pushed back

Dealers in the United States reacted soon after the announcement. The Virginia Automobile Dealers Association wrote that Volvo's proposed scheme violates its retailer agreement with dealers, and that the agreement does not contemplate anything like the scheme attributed to Volvo officials. Dealer associations in California and Virginia said the plan threatened the traditional franchise dealer model. A Memphis dealer owner said the message from Sweden implied that dealerships would become distribution and service centers.

Volvo's U.S. chief executive, Anders Gustafsson, answered that there were "some misunderstandings out there." He described the online model as a shopping and research tool for customers. Volvo's announcement said dealers would still handle selling, preparing, delivering, and servicing the cars.

The same reporting added two pieces of history. Dealer associations for other brands had tried to overturn Tesla's direct sales model, with some success in certain states. And dealer lobbyists had persuaded the California DMV to force Volvo to stop its Care by Volvo subscription program in that state. Disputes like these are decided by state franchise law and by the contracts between brands and dealers, which is why one announcement can lead to very different outcomes in different places.

## Fixed prices and the commission question

Talk of transparent, set prices points to what the industry calls the agency model. Steve Young, who writes the ICDP chairman's blog, wrote about it right after the Volvo news. Under agency, the manufacturer sets the price and the dealer earns a commission instead of a sales margin. Under the traditional franchise model, the dealer and customer negotiate from the sticker price, and the dealer is rewarded through margin and bonus systems.

Young ran a simple what-if exercise with UK dealer group data. If net revenue from new car sales were replaced with a 5 percent commission, about 1,400 euros per car, total dealer group revenue would fall by about 40 percent. A business with 1 billion euros in turnover would become one with about 600 million. At the same time, return on sales would rise from 0.8 percent to 1.4 percent, if the commission fairly replaced the old margin and bonus payments.

:::note What the ICDP exercise is and is not
Young described his numbers as an illustration from a "what if" exercise. They are not a published industry forecast, and they use UK data, so U.S. dealers under state franchise law may see something different.
:::

## What stays with the dealership

By Volvo's own description, the retailer would still sell, prepare, deliver, and service the cars. That is a reminder that a dealership earns money in more than one place. A change in how new cars are priced affects one part of the business, and the rest of the store keeps running on its own economics.

## Questions worth asking about any brand's retail plan

When a manufacturer announces a new way to sell, a few plain questions help sort the news. Is it a plan, a pilot, or a change already in effect? Who sets the price, and how is the dealer paid? What do the franchise agreement and state law say about it? Has the target been revised since, as Volvo's 2030 target was? And how much of a dealership's income depends on the part of the business that is changing?

Reading the original announcement, the dealer response, and later updates together gives a better picture than any single headline. The Volvo story shows why: a 2021 statement, a pointed reply from dealers, and a revised target three years later.
