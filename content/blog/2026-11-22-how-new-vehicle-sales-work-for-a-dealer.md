A franchised dealership sells new vehicles for the brands it holds a franchise for. How those vehicles reach the lot, how they are paid for and how they are sold to customers shapes the whole business. This article walks through that path in plain steps. It is written for readers who are learning how dealerships work, including people who are thinking about investing in the sector.

## The franchise agreement comes first

A franchised dealership does not simply buy and sell any new vehicle it likes. In its annual report to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, AutoNation, a large public dealer group, says it operates each of its new vehicle stores under a franchise agreement with a vehicle manufacturer or distributor.

The same report says these agreements give the store a non-exclusive right to sell that manufacturer's brand of vehicles and to offer related parts and service within a specified market area. In practice, that means a dealer's new vehicle business is tied to the brands named in its agreements. A store with a Chevrolet franchise sells new Chevrolets. It does not sell new vehicles from a brand it has no agreement with.

## Where the new vehicles come from

AutoNation describes two ways its franchised stores get new vehicles for retail sale. The first is directly from the manufacturer or distributor. The second is through dealer trades with other stores of the same brand franchise.

The report also says the company relies exclusively on vehicle manufacturers for its new vehicle inventory. It lists the manufacturers' ability to design, build and allocate an attractive product mix to its stores at the right time as a factor that affects its results. Put simply, a dealer does not fully control what shows up on its lot. The manufacturer decides how many vehicles of each kind a store receives.

:::note Worth knowing
AutoNation's annual report states that it relies exclusively on vehicle manufacturers for its new vehicle inventory, and that dealer trades with other stores of the same brand are a second way to get vehicles.
:::

## How the inventory is paid for

A lot full of new vehicles is a large amount of money tied up in stock. Dealers commonly carry that stock with floorplan financing, which is borrowing used to hold vehicle inventory until the vehicles sell.

AutoNation reports a cost it calls new vehicle inventory carrying expense. It describes this as the floorplan interest expense on new vehicles, net of floorplan assistance that it receives from automotive manufacturers. The report also says manufacturers generally support their franchised dealers with direct financial assistance, and floorplan assistance is one of the areas it names.

The practical point is time. A new vehicle that sits unsold keeps carrying interest. A dealer that sells vehicles quickly carries less of that cost than a dealer whose inventory sits.

## Selling the vehicle to a customer

NADA, the National Automobile Dealers Association, publishes industry figures every year. In its midyear report for 2026, NADA says the average franchised dealership sold 464 new vehicles in the first half of the year, at an average retail price of $48,990. It also reports that new light-vehicle sales fell 2.6 percent compared with the first half of 2025, and that it expects dealers to sell 16.0 million new light-vehicle units in 2026.

The same report covers 16,991 franchised light-vehicle dealerships nationwide. Those numbers are averages across the whole industry. Any single store can sell more or fewer vehicles than the average.

:::note Worth knowing
NADA's 2026 midyear report puts new vehicle sales at 53.6 percent of total franchised dealership sales dollars, down from 54.2 percent in 2025.
:::

## How big new vehicle sales are inside a dealership

New vehicles are the largest share of a dealership's sales dollars, but they are not the only share. In the same NADA report, used vehicles were 32.8 percent of total sales dollars, and service, parts and body shop were 13.6 percent.

Notice what this measures. These figures are shares of sales dollars. They do not show how much profit each department keeps. A department that brings in a large share of sales dollars is not automatically the largest share of profit, and the NADA figures do not say either way. When you study a dealership, look at what each department sells and how it is described, and be careful about reading profit into a sales figure.

## What this means when you study a dealership

If you are learning how dealerships work, a few questions come out of the facts above. Which brands does the dealership hold franchises for, and how much does it depend on each manufacturer? How does the manufacturer's allocation affect the vehicles the store can sell? How is inventory financed, and what does carrying it cost? How much of the store's sales comes from outside new vehicles?

None of these questions has a single right answer, and the answers differ from store to store. They are a starting point for reading a dealer group's own reports, which is where the details about its brands, its inventory and its financing are found. A public company such as AutoNation files those details with the SEC, and reading one is a good way to see how a real dealer group describes its own operations.
