A car dealership sells vehicles, but it also fixes them, and it does both from one fixed address. A business that sells online can serve customers in many places from a single warehouse. A franchised dealership cannot. Its land, building and surrounding community stay where they are, so the question of where it sits comes up early whenever someone studies the business.

This article explains in plain terms what a market area is, how traffic and access shape a dealership's day, and what to look at when you judge a dealership's location. It is general education about the industry.

## What a market area is

A market area is the geography a dealership draws its customers from. Picture the homes and workplaces within a reasonable drive of the lot. A dealership in a small town may serve several nearby towns because it is the closest store for its brand. A dealership in a large metro area usually shares its area with many other stores and has to win customers who could drive to any of them.

NADA, the trade association for franchised dealers, says most dealership employees live within driving distance of the store. The same drive-time logic applies to customers as a general rule of retail: people tend to buy and service vehicles near where they live and work.

Market areas are not drawn on an official map. They are a way of thinking about who can realistically reach the store, and they differ from one dealership to the next.

## Competition inside the market area

NADA says about 18,000 local new-car dealerships compete for more than 17 million new-car buyers a year. It also says that when more than one dealer of the same brand operates in a market, those dealers compete with each other.

That makes the neighbors part of the location story. A dealership with no other store of its brand nearby faces a different situation from one with two same-brand stores a few miles away. The mix of brands matters too, because a market that already has several strong brands is a different place to sell than one where a brand is thinly represented.

:::note The numbers behind the industry
NADA reports 16,990 franchised light-vehicle dealerships in its 2025 full-year data, with total sales of $1.3 trillion. Its 2025 report also finds that 90.5 percent of dealership owners operate between one and five dealerships.
:::

## Traffic and access

Traffic means two things. One is the vehicles that pass the property each day, which gives the lot visibility. The other is the customers who actually turn in. A dealership on a busy road is easy to see, but it still has to be easy to enter and leave. A lot that is hard to reach from the road, or sits behind a confusing intersection, can lose visits that would otherwise happen.

Access also affects the service department. A person buys a vehicle a few times in a lifetime, but the same person may come back for maintenance and repairs many times. A convenient address makes those return visits easier, and return visits are what keep a service lane busy.

## Why location matters beyond the showroom

It is easy to think of a dealership as a place that sells new cars. NADA's 2025 data shows a broader picture. Of total franchised dealership sales, new vehicles made up 54.9 percent, used vehicles 31.8 percent, and service and parts 13.3 percent. Service and parts came to about $164.6 billion, with more than 276 million repair orders.

Repair orders are visits. Each one is a customer coming back to the same address. For that reason a dealership's location affects more than how many vehicles it sells on a given Saturday. It affects how easily the store can keep customers returning year after year.

## Land, buildings and the property itself

A dealership needs a large lot to display inventory, a showroom, and service bays. NADA says dealerships have invested more than $200 billion in land, buildings and infrastructure in their communities.

That is why real estate is part of any serious look at a dealership. Two questions come up quickly. The first is whether the property is owned or leased, and on what terms. The second is whether the site suits both sales and service, including parking, bay access and room for customers' vehicles. The property can matter on its own terms, apart from the car business that operates on it.

## Local knowledge and local owners

Because 90.5 percent of owners run between one and five dealerships, most dealerships are managed by operators who know their local communities. That local knowledge shows up in practical decisions, such as which community events to take part in, how to hire from the surrounding area, and how to adjust to the local economy.

A community with a mix of employers behaves differently from one that depends heavily on one industry. A dealership is also an employer, and NADA says each one employs an average of 67 people, most of whom live nearby. The relationship runs both ways: the dealership needs the community, and the community gains from a stable local employer.

## Questions to ask about any dealership location

When you look at a dealership, a few plain questions are useful. Who lives within a reasonable drive, and how many of them are potential buyers or service customers? Which other stores sell the same brand nearby, and how far away are they? How easy is it to reach the property from the main roads? Does the site have enough room for both sales and service? Is the land owned or leased? How diverse is the local economy?

None of these questions has a single right answer. Together they give a clearer picture of the market a dealership works in, and they explain why two stores with the same brand can perform very differently.
