A car dealership looks like one business from the street. An annual report shows it as several lines of business that share the same customers, the same building and the same staff. This article reads one publicly filed report to see how those lines connect.

The report is the Form 10-K that AutoNation, Inc. filed for fiscal year 2025. AutoNation is a public company, so it must describe its operations to the Securities and Exchange Commission in plain terms. Other dealer groups differ in size and brands, but the basic lines of business are the same across franchised dealers. We pair the filing with the 2025 full-year report from the National Automobile Dealers Association (NADA) to show how large the lines are across the whole industry.

## The lines of business named in the filing

The 10-K describes four kinds of activity: new vehicle sales, used vehicle sales, parts and service, and finance and insurance. It also reports a separate captive finance company, which provides financing on vehicles the company sells. That last piece is specific to a large group like AutoNation, so this article sticks to the four lines that a typical franchised dealer has.

The filing groups its stores into Domestic, Import and Premium Luxury segments and says that the franchises in each of these segments also sell used vehicles, parts, and repair and maintenance services. In other words, a franchise is not only a place that sells new cars. Each store carries all of the lines.

## Where the new vehicles come from

For new vehicles, the filing says its franchised stores acquire them "either directly from the applicable automotive manufacturer or distributor or through dealer trades." The dealer does not build the product. It buys from the automaker, or from another dealer, and sells to the customer.

The segment table in the filing shows the scale for one group. For fiscal year 2025 it lists new vehicle revenue of about $13.5 billion, 259,264 retail new vehicle units and 323 franchises across the three segments. The point for a reader is the unit count. Dealers count vehicles sold as well as dollars, because each vehicle sold can lead to the other lines described below.

## How used vehicles are fed by trade-ins

The filing says the company generally acquires used vehicles "from customers, primarily through trade-ins and our 'We'll Buy Your Car' program." Vehicles that are not sold at retail are generally sold wholesale through auctions.

This is the first link between lines. A customer who buys a new vehicle and trades in the old one is a purchase on the new side and a source of inventory on the used side, in the same visit. Used inventory does not come only from auctions. A large part comes from people the dealer is already talking to.

## How service and parts connect to the rest

The filing says the company generally reconditions used vehicles it acquires for retail sale in its own parts and service departments. So the service department is not a separate shop that happens to share a lot. It prepares the used vehicles that the sales side then sells.

The filing also says the company aims to encourage customers "to bring their vehicles to our stores for all of their vehicle service, maintenance, and collision repair needs." It describes this parts and service business, which it calls After-Sales, as higher-margin and as having historically been less sensitive to macroeconomic conditions than vehicle sales. That is the company's own description of its business, and it is a statement about the past, not a forecast.

:::note Worth knowing
NADA reports that dealerships wrote more than 276 million repair orders in 2025, with service and parts sales exceeding $164 billion. Total franchised light-vehicle dealership sales topped $1.3 trillion. By simple division, service and parts is about one dollar in eight of total sales.
:::

## Where finance and insurance sit

The filing says the company primarily arranges financing through third-party lenders and earns a commission for doing so. It also sells vehicle protection products on a commission basis. These are the products customers are offered in the finance office when they buy a vehicle.

This line depends on the vehicle sale. A customer has to buy a vehicle, new or used, before financing or protection products come up. That makes it the clearest example of one line riding on another. The filing also notes that income from arranging financing is expected to shift over time toward the company's captive finance company, which is a reminder that the way a line is earned can change while the line stays in place.

## The pattern across the lines

Reading the pieces together gives a simple picture. A customer buys a new vehicle from stock the dealer got from the automaker. The trade-in becomes used inventory. The service department reconditions that inventory and later services the vehicle the customer bought. The finance office arranges the loan and offers protection products on the sale. Each step involves the same customer or the same vehicle, so the lines are tied together.

That is why a single line cannot be understood alone. A change in new vehicle supply affects trade-ins. A change in trade-ins affects used inventory. Service volume depends on how many vehicles the dealer sold and how many owners come back.

## What to look for in any dealer report

When you read a dealer group's annual report, check how it describes each line, where the vehicles in each line come from, and which lines depend on a vehicle sale. Look for statements about the past, such as the After-Sales description above, and keep them separate from forecasts. Compare any figure you want to rely on with an industry source such as NADA.
