When people picture a car dealership, they picture the showroom. New cars on the floor, a used lot out front, a salesperson with a clipboard. That picture is accurate, but it leaves out a department that runs all day in the back of the building: service and parts.

This post compares the size of that department with vehicle sales. It uses two public sources, the National Automobile Dealers Association (NADA) full-year data report for 2025 and the annual report of a public dealer group. The goal is to show how a dealership's sales are divided, and why the division matters to anyone trying to understand the business.

## The three big departments

A franchised dealership reports its sales by department. NADA's 2025 report breaks total dealership sales into three groups: new vehicles, used vehicles, and service and parts.

Across all dealerships in 2025, new vehicles made up 54.9 percent of sales dollars. Used vehicles made up 31.8 percent. Service and parts made up 13.3 percent. The year before, the split was 54.7, 32.0 and 13.2 percent.

Total dealership sales in 2025 came to about $1.3 trillion, according to the same report. Service and parts sales were about $164.6 billion of that total, or roughly $9.7 million for the average dealership.

:::note Worth knowing
NADA reports that service and parts accounted for 13.3 percent of total dealership sales dollars in 2025, up slightly from 13.2 percent in 2024.
:::

## Why service and parts looks small and still matters

At 13.3 percent of sales dollars, service and parts is the smallest of the three departments. If size by sales dollars were the only measure, it would look like a side business.

It is not a side business. Nearly $165 billion in annual sales is a very large number by any standard. And a department's share of sales is only one way to look at it. Sales dollars say how much money came through the door. They do not say how much of it the dealership kept.

The two kinds of work are different, which is why dealer groups look at the departments in terms of profit as well as sales.

The NADA report we used here does not include gross profit figures by department, so this post does not make a claim about exact profit shares. It is worth keeping the distinction in mind whenever you see a dealership's numbers: share of sales and share of profit are different measures.

## What a public dealer group says about it

Public companies must describe their businesses in their annual filings. AutoNation, a public dealer group, describes its parts and service business in its 10-K for fiscal year 2025.

In that filing, the company refers to its "higher-margin parts and service business" and says it has historically been less sensitive to macroeconomic conditions than new and used vehicle sales. That is the company's own description, and it is a claim from one company, not an industry average.

The same filing reports that AutoNation sold 259,264 new retail units in 2025 for $13,501.3 million in new vehicle revenue. That figure helps you see the scale of the vehicle side of a large group. The portion of the filing we reviewed did not include revenue or profit figures for the other departments, so we do not compare them here.

## Vehicle sales are tied to the market

New and used vehicle sales depend on things outside the dealership's control: interest rates, consumer confidence, manufacturer supply and pricing. NADA reports that the average new vehicle retail selling price in 2025 was $48,205, up from $47,652 in 2024. A dealership sold an average of 955 new vehicles in 2025, up from 935 in 2024.

When prices move or demand softens, the vehicle departments feel it first. That is one reason it helps to look at how much of a dealership's business does not depend on vehicle sales.

## What to take from the comparison

Put the facts together and a simple picture forms. Vehicle sales are the largest share of a dealership's sales dollars, with new and used together making up about 87 percent. Service and parts is a smaller share, at 13.3 percent, but it is a very large business in total dollars and, by the dealer group's own description, a higher-margin one that has been less sensitive to the economy.

If you are learning how dealerships work, or trying to understand what an investor in dealerships would look at, this is a good place to begin. Look at the whole store, not just the showroom. Ask how each department contributes to sales, and then ask separately how each contributes to profit.

## One more note on reading dealership numbers

Industry averages describe the average store. An individual dealership can look quite different, depending on its brand, its market and how it runs its departments. Any real decision should be based on the numbers for the dealership in question.
