Two car lots can sit across the street from each other and run on very different rules. One may have a brand name on the building and a contract with a car maker. The other may sell used cars it bought at auction. Both are called dealerships, but the way they get cars, make money and face competition is not the same. This article explains the two models in plain terms.

## What a franchised dealership is

A franchised dealership sells new vehicles of one or more brands under a written agreement with the manufacturer. AutoNation, a large publicly traded dealer group, describes this in its annual report. Each new vehicle store operates under a franchise agreement that grants a non-exclusive right to sell the brand and to offer related parts and service within a specified market area. It also gives the store the right to use the manufacturer's trademarks.

That is why a franchised store carries the maker's name, signs and colors. The store is not owned by the manufacturer. It is a separate business that has been granted the right to represent the brand.

## What an independent dealership is

An independent dealership has no such agreement. Mercer Capital, a valuation firm that studies the auto retail industry, puts it this way: independent dealers do not have formal agreements with the vehicle manufacturer. As a result, independent dealers do not retail new vehicles. Their business is used vehicles.

Many independents focus on a niche. Mercer Capital names three common ones: high-end or classic cars, middle-market used cars, and buy here pay here lots, which finance the customer directly.

## Where each kind gets its cars

A franchised store gets new vehicles from the manufacturer or distributor, or through trades with other stores of the same brand. AutoNation states that it relies exclusively on the manufacturers for its new vehicle inventory. That means the maker has a lot of say over how many cars a store receives and under what incentive programs.

Both kinds of dealer buy used vehicles. Mercer Capital notes that both segments source used inventory from auctions and trade-ins. For an independent lot, used inventory is the whole business. For a franchised store, it is one department among several.

## What the contract requires

A franchise agreement comes with obligations. AutoNation's report says the agreements set requirements for inventory and working capital levels, the sales process, marketing and branding, facilities, signage, personnel, changes in management, and monthly financial reporting.

Terms vary. The report says they range from one year to no expiration date, and some manufacturers have committed to renew a franchise if the store complies with the agreement. A manufacturer can end or decline to renew an agreement for reasons that include weak sales volume, sales effectiveness, and customer satisfaction. The report adds that state dealership franchise laws generally make this hard to do outside of bankruptcy, because they require good cause.

An independent dealer is free of this contract. It is also free of the protection the contract brings.

## How competition differs

Manufacturers generally set marketing areas where only one franchised dealer of a brand may operate. State franchise laws also restrict a manufacturer from relocating a franchise, or opening a same-brand store, in another dealer's area without approval. In practice this limits how many stores of the same brand can compete head to head in one area.

Independent dealers do not get this. Mercer Capital says they have no area protection and face more competition from both other independents and franchised dealers. A used car lot can open on almost any corner, and anyone selling used cars is a possible rival.

## The departments inside the building

Mercer Capital describes franchised dealers as having four core departments: new vehicle sales, used vehicle sales, service and parts, and finance and insurance. Independent dealers center on used sales. Some offer service and parts, but it is usually basic. Mercer Capital lists oil changes, tires, and work to get vehicles ready for resale.

This is one of the clearest practical differences. A franchised store has more than one source of revenue in the same building. An independent lot usually depends on the used car it sells and the financing attached to it.

## How each is financed

Dealers pay for their inventory with floor plan financing, which is a line of credit that funds the cars on the lot. Mercer Capital says franchised dealers can use the manufacturer's financing arm, such as Ford Motor Credit or Toyota Credit. Independent dealers rely on regional banks and specialty firms such as NextGear Capital, and their floor plan limits tend to be lower.

Collections matter more to independents that finance customers directly. Mercer Capital notes that collections and repossessions are especially important to independent and buy here pay here dealers, who serve lower-income buyers.

## How many of each

Counts of independent dealers vary a lot by who is counted. Mercer Capital reports estimates of about 27,000 to 30,000, a figure that includes individuals who file with the IRS as used car dealers. Other estimates of physical independent used car dealers run from 16,000 to 17,000. The difference comes from the definition, not from a disagreement about the market.

## Which one is which

When you want to tell the two apart, look at the contract and the cars. A franchised dealership has an agreement with the manufacturer, sells new vehicles of the brand, runs a service department and gets its new inventory from the maker. An independent dealership has no manufacturer agreement, sells used vehicles, and buys its inventory at auctions and from trade-ins.
